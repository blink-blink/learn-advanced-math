1. One idea behind the whole semester #

In plain words

This whole course is one trick, used over and over: when something is a tangled mess, look at it from a different set of coordinates so the tangle disappears. A matrix can be all mixed together — every number leaning on every other number. Switch to the right coordinates and every piece just gets multiplied by one number, with nothing mixing at all. Each chapter is a different way of finding those coordinates, plus what to do on the rare occasion they don't exist.

Every chapter in this review is the same move, played on a different stage:

\[ \textbf{Find the basis in which the operator becomes simple (diagonal).} \]

A linear map \(A\) looks complicated in the standard basis. It mixes every coordinate into every other. But in the basis of its eigenvectors, each coordinate is merely multiplied by its own number: \(D=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_i)\). Everything in this review is machinery for finding, exploiting, or generalizing that basis — and of the operator it applies to:

  • §1–§3 build the language: what a "basis" even is, how matrices represent maps, and what "changing basis" formally means, namely \(B=P^{-1}AP\).
  • §4–§5 find the special basis (eigenvalues/vectors), use it (diagonalization, \(A^k=PD^kP^{-1}\)), and when it doesn't exist, fall back to the SVD, the "diagonalization for everyone."
  • §6–§7 discover that differentiation is a linear operator on a function space, whose "eigenvectors" are exponentials: \(\frac{d}{dt}e^{rt}=r\,e^{rt}\). Solving \(y''+3y'+2y=0\) is literally an eigenvalue problem for \(\frac{d}{dt}\). That's why the "characteristic equation" has "characteristic polynomial" in its name.
  • §8 the Laplace transform is the change-of-basis that turns differentiation into multiplication by \(s\).
  • §9 Fourier is the eigenbasis of differentiation restricted to periodic functions, sines and cosines, and it is an infinite-dimensional Gram–Schmidt (§1) applied to functions.

Hold that one sentence during the exam: “diagonalize” is what every method on this site secretly does. When a problem feels like a bag of tricks, ask "what is the operator here, and what are its eigenvectors?"

2. Master timeline: why this review is in this order #

In plain words

None of this is new. Some of it is about eighteen hundred years older than the computer you're reading it on — Chinese scribes were solving linear systems around the year 200 AD, and the people who invented calculus did it specifically so they could write down an equation for motion. The chapters are ordered roughly the way the ideas were actually discovered, so the course reads as a story with a plot: letters first, then numbers, then the shapes that pull it all together.

Master timeline. Scan the years, ignore the details on a first pass — this is here so you can place each chapter in time, and so the "why does this exist?" question always has an answer.
  • ~200 ADThe Chinese Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art solves linear systems by a procedure identical to Gaussian elimination. 1650 years early.
  • 1670sNewton and Leibniz invent calculus explicitly to write differential equations.
  • 1740sEuler analyzes rigid-body rotation, finds the principal axes (proto-eigenvectors) and the method for constant-coefficient ODEs.
  • 1750Cramer publishes determinants-for-systems.
  • 1780sLaplace's transform appears in probability and celestial mechanics.
  • 1807/1822Fourier claims heat profiles are sine series; Lagrange objects (both partly right).
  • 1829Cauchy systematizes symmetric eigenvalue theory; Dirichlet fixes Fourier's convergence claim.
  • 1844–88Grassmann's vector spaces (1844), Cayley's matrix algebra (1858), Peano's axioms (1888) — the abstraction finally catches up with the computations.
  • 1872Klein's Erlangen program: a geometry is the invariants of a transformation group.
  • 1880s–90sPoincaré invents phase-plane qualitative theory; Lyapunov makes stability rigorous.
  • 1898Gibbs explains the Fourier overshoot.
  • 1900sHilbert pushes eigen-theory to infinite dimensions; quantum mechanics will use the word eigenvalue verbatim.

A standard syllabus compresses roughly 300 years of mathematics into one semester, in essentially the order it was discovered.

History in five lines. Grassmann (1844, Die lineale Ausdehnungslehre) proposed n-dimensional "extensive quantities", so far ahead of its notation that it was ignored for 45 years (Gibbs discovered him only in 1877). Gibbs & Heaviside (1880s) independently cut Hamilton's quaternions down to the 3-D dot/cross product engineers actually use. Peano (1888) distilled all of it into the eight axioms you could quote today. Banach & Hilbert (1920s) ran the same axioms on infinite-dimensional function spaces, the space where Fourier series secretly live: sines are "vectors," and integrals of products are "dot products."

1. What a vector space is; the subspace test #

In plain words

Some places let you add things together and multiply them by plain numbers without anything ever breaking. Arrows do that. A list of numbers does that. A grid of numbers does that — that's a matrix. A recipe for a curve does that — that's a polynomial. Calling all of them vector spaces is not a restriction — it's a shortcut, because every rule in the rest of the course works identically in all of them. You learn it once, you can use it everywhere.

A subspace is just a vector space hiding inside a bigger one, with the same three-part test every time.

A vector space \(V\) over \(\mathbb{R}\) is a set with \(+\) and \(\cdot\) obeying 8 axioms: associativity and commutativity of addition, an additive identity \(0\) and inverses, and the scalar-distribution/associativity/unit laws. The point of the axioms: anything that satisfies them behaves like arrows, so a theorem proved once applies everywhere. Examples on exams, with the reason each qualifies:

  • \(\mathbb{R}^n\), the prototype.
  • \(M_{m\times n}\): all \(m\times n\) matrices are vectors, add them, scale them, the axioms hold.
  • \(P_n\): polynomials of degree \(\le n\), dimension \(n+1\), basis \(\{1,x,x^2,\dots,x^n\}\) (careful: \(n+1\), the constant term is a dimension).
  • \(C[a,b]\): continuous functions on \([a,b]\), infinite-dimensional; the arena of §9.
  • Every solution set of a homogeneous linear ODE: if \(y_1,y_2\) solve \(y''+py'+qy=0\), so does \(cy_1+dy_2\) (the differential operator is linear). This is why "general solution \(=C_1y_1+C_2y_2\)" is secretly a statement about bases of a 2-dimensional solution space. §6 will repeat this in disguise, and §8 too.

Subspace test, a nonempty \(W\subseteq V\) is a subspace iff (a) \(0\in W\), (b) closed under \(+\), (c) closed under scalar multiplication. In \(\mathbb{R}^3\) the subspaces are exactly: the origin, lines through the origin, planes through the origin, and all of \(\mathbb{R}^3\). Anything else (a shifted line, the unit circle, the first quadrant) fails at least one check, practice naming which.

Why \(0\in W\) is the fastest disproof: if \(W\) is closed under scalar mult. and nonempty, then \(0\cdot w=0\in W\) for free, so a set missing the origin (like the affine line \(y=2x+1\)) fails automatically. Check (a) first, always.

2. Span, linear independence, basis, dimension #

In plain words

You can't list every point in a space — there are too many. So you pick a few generators instead, and then everything in the space is a recipe using those. Three words, three questions:

  • Span — what can I build from these?
  • Linear independence — is any of them wasting its place?
  • Basis — both at once. And dimension is how many it takes, which no choice of list can change.
  • Span: \(\mathrm{span}\{v_1,\dots,v_k\}=\{\sum c_iv_i\}\), automatically the smallest subspace containing them (proof: it's closed, and any subspace containing the \(v_i\)'s must contain every combination).
  • Linear independence: \(c_1v_1+\dots+c_kv_k=0\Rightarrow\) every \(c_i=0\). In practice: stack as columns of \(A\); independent \(\iff \mathrm{rank}A=k\) \(\iff\) \(Ax=0\) has only the trivial solution. Geometrically: nobody is redundant, no \(v_i\) is buildable from the rest.
  • Basis = independent + spans \(V\). Dimension = the (uniquely determined) size of any basis. In \(\mathbb{R}^n\): any \(n\) independent vectors are automatically a basis. You only check one property.
The mental model. Independence answers how few vectors describe the space without redundancy; spanning answers is that enough? A basis is the sweet spot of both. And when later chapters say "choose coordinates," they mean "choose a basis" — the same physical vector has different number-tuples in different bases, which is exactly what §3.3's change-of-basis and §5's diagonalization manipulate.

3. Inner products, norms, Gram–Schmidt #

In plain words

So far a vector is just a bag of numbers with no sense of size or direction — you can add and scale it, but you can't say how long it is or what angle it points at. Add one extra rule, the inner product (multiply matching parts, add them up), and geometry returns: length, angle, and "perpendicular."

Gram–Schmidt is the machine that does the straightening, and it is the single most reused step in the course: §5's QR factorization, §5's least squares, and §9's Fourier coefficients are all this same machine run on different inputs.

\[ u\cdot v=\sum_i u_iv_i,\qquad \|v\|=\sqrt{v\cdot v},\qquad \cos\theta=\frac{u\cdot v}{\|u\|\,\|v\|} \]

Orthogonal means \(u\cdot v=0\). An orthogonal set of nonzero vectors is automatically independent (dot the combination with \(v_i\) to isolate \(c_i\)). Gram–Schmidt converts any basis into an orthogonal one by subtracting, step by step, the projection onto directions already accepted:

\[ u_1=v_1,\qquad u_{k}=v_k-\sum_{j<k}\frac{v_k\cdot u_j}{u_j\cdot u_j}\,u_j,\qquad e_i=\frac{u_i}{\|u_i\|} \]

Where this goes: applied to the columns of a matrix, Gram–Schmidt is the QR factorization \(A=QR\) (Q orthonormal columns, R upper-triangular) — the numerically stable route to least squares, which is why ML libraries avoid forming \(A^TA\) by hand: it squares your conditioning error.

And it comes back one last time in §9. The projection formula \(\mathrm{proj}_u\,v=\frac{v\cdot u}{u\cdot u}u\) reappears, word for word, as the Euler coefficient formula of Fourier series: same projection, infinitely many directions instead of two.
CS uses of this chapter.
  • Embeddings: a document/user/image is a point in \(\mathbb{R}^n\), and cosine similarity is the \(\cos\theta\) above.
  • Pixels: pixels \(\in\mathbb{R}^3\), and RGB→grayscale is one linear functional — the BT.601 matrix \([0.299,0.587,0.114]\) lives in every JPEG encoder.
  • Coding theory: valid codewords of a linear block code are exactly \(\ker H\), a subspace. That is why "XOR two valid codewords gives another valid codeword," and why syndrome decoding works.
  • Circuits: Kirchhoff's current laws are linear constraints, so the space of legal current assignments is a null space — and its dimension is the number of independent loops. Euler's formula for graphs, hiding in an EE course.

4. The four fundamental subspaces #

In plain words

A matrix splits the world in two, twice over. On one side are the directions it can produce; on the other, the directions it flattens to nothing. The surprising part is that the same two questions can be asked of the matrix and of its transpose, which gives four spaces instead of two — and every one of their sizes is readable straight off the row-reduced matrix, by counting pivots and free variables.

Once you have the four, the question "does \(Ax=b\) have a solution?" stops being an elimination slog and becomes a one-line test: is \(b\) standing at right angles to the left null space?

For \(A\) of size \(m\times n\), rank \(r\) (Strang's picture, draw it once and \(Ax=b\) forever makes sense):

SubspaceSits inDimensionBasis from
Column space \(C(A)\)\(\mathbb{R}^m\)\(r\)pivot columns of \(A\) (of \(A\), not of \(U\)!)
Null space \(N(A)\)\(\mathbb{R}^n\)\(n-r\)free-variable special solutions of \(Ax=0\)
Row space \(C(A^T)\)\(\mathbb{R}^n\)\(r\)pivot rows of the echelon form \(U\)
Left null space \(N(A^T)\)\(\mathbb{R}^m\)\(m-r\)zero rows of the elimination matrix \(E\)

Rank–nullity: \(\mathrm{rank}+\mathrm{nullity}=n\), the domain splits into "directions that survive independently" + "directions crushed to 0." The deep fact: \(C(A^T)\perp N(A)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^n\), and \(C(A)\perp N(A^T)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^m\), every linear map carves both spaces into orthogonal pairs.

Why you'll care later. \(Ax=b\) is solvable \(\iff\) \(b\) is orthogonal to every vector in \(N(A^T)\) — the Fredholm alternative, which is just a dressed-up way of saying "the equations can't contradict each other." And least squares (§5) works by finding the closest reachable \(b\) inside \(C(A)\), by projection. Gram–Schmidt again.

6. Worked examples #

In plain words

Four problems, each carried all the way to a finished answer: is it a subspace?, are these vectors independent?, straighten a basis, and find every solution of \(Ax=0\). Open them one at a time and check every line yourself — the arithmetic is deliberately unhurried, because on an exam the arithmetic is where the marks are won or lost.

Example 1 · subspace triage (three sets, one-line verdicts)
In plain words

Three sets, one question: is it a vector space? Run the three-part subspace test on each — and watch how fast the very first check (does it contain the origin?) kills the second one outright.

  • \(S=\{(x,y):y=2x\}\): \(0\in S\); \(c_1(x_1,2x_1)+c_2(x_2,2x_2)=(c_1x_1+c_2x_2,\;2(c_1x_1+c_2x_2))\in S\). ✔ subspace (a line through the origin; basis \(\{(1,2)\}\), dim 1).
  • \(S'=\{(x,y):y=2x+1\}\): \((0,0)\notin S'\). ✘ not a subspace; it's a coset of one (an affine line, parallel to a subspace but shifted).
  • \(S''=\{p\in P_2 : p(3)=0\}\): evaluation at 3 is a linear functional, so its kernel is a subspace ✔. By the factor theorem every such \(p\) is \((x-3)(a+bx)\), so a basis is \(\{x-3,(x-3)^2\}\), dim 2, not 3: one condition = one lost dimension. (Same bookkeeping as rank–nullity: the map \(p\mapsto p(3)\) has rank 1, nullity \(\dim P_2-1=2\).)
Example 2 · independence via rank (with the elimination written out)
In plain words

Stack three vectors as columns, row-reduce, and count the pivots. Three pivots means nobody is redundant, so they're independent.

Are \(v_1=(1,2,1),\;v_2=(2,5,3),\;v_3=(1,1,-1)\) independent? Stack and eliminate: \(\begin{bmatrix}1&2&1\\2&5&3\\1&1&-1\end{bmatrix}\xrightarrow{R_2-2R_1,\;R_3-R_1}\begin{bmatrix}1&2&1\\0&1&1\\0&-1&-2\end{bmatrix}\xrightarrow{R_3+R_2}\begin{bmatrix}1&2&1\\0&1&1\\0&0&-1\end{bmatrix}\). Three pivots → rank 3 → independent → a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^3\). Had the last row become all-zero, they'd span a plane and one vector would be a combination of the others. Side note: the elimination shows \(\det=\) product of pivots \(=-1\neq0\), the §2 "equivalence chain" in a single computation.

Example 3 · Gram–Schmidt with real numbers, then use it
In plain words

The same machine as Visual 2, done with actual arithmetic instead of symbols. Then look at what fell out: a 45° rotation. Worth doing by hand once, because after this you never have to wonder about the signs again.

\(v_1=(1,1),\;v_2=(0,2)\). \(u_1=v_1\). \(u_2=v_2-\frac{v_2\cdot u_1}{u_1\cdot u_1}u_1=(0,2)-\frac{2}{2}(1,1)=(-1,1)\). Check \(u_1\cdot u_2=-1+1=0\) ✔. Normalize: \(e_1=(\tfrac1{\sqrt2},\tfrac1{\sqrt2}),\;e_2=(-\tfrac1{\sqrt2},\tfrac1{\sqrt2})\). The matrix \(Q=[e_1\;e_2]=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\) satisfies \(Q^TQ=I\). It's a rotation by 45°. Orthonormal bases are exactly "coordinate systems related by rotations/reflections": which is the reason SVD's \(U,V\) (orthogonal columns) can be read as pure rotations in §5's morph.

Example 4 · a full null-space computation (the workhorse of the chapter)
In plain words

The one calculation this chapter exists for. Reduce the matrix, spot the free variables, then write one clean vector per free variable with the pivot entries filled in to match. The same pattern — pivot columns for one space, special solutions for the other — returns in nearly every later chapter.

\(A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&0&3\\2&4&1&7\\1&2&1&4\end{bmatrix}\xrightarrow{R_2-2R_1,\,R_3-R_1}\begin{bmatrix}1&2&0&3\\0&0&1&1\\0&0&1&1\end{bmatrix}\xrightarrow{R_3-R_2}\begin{bmatrix}1&2&0&3\\0&0&1&1\\0&0&0&0\end{bmatrix}\). Pivots in columns 1 and 3; free variables \(x_2,x_4\). Solve for pivots: \(x_1=-2x_2-3x_4,\;x_3=-x_4\). So \[ N(A)=\mathrm{span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix}-2\\1\\0\\0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}-3\\0\\-1\\1\end{bmatrix}\right\} \] Read it: "every solution is exactly a combination of these two, nothing else." Rank 2 + nullity 2 = 4 = n ✔. The same pattern, pivot columns give the basis of the column space, special solutions give the null space, is the whole answer to "describe all solutions of \(Ax=0\)." In §6 it will be re-skinned as "the homogeneous solution of an ODE," in §8 as "the free response."

Pitfalls.
  • "Closed under \(+\) and scalar mult." must hold for all real scalars: the first quadrant is closed under \(+\) and positive scaling but fails at \(c=-1\).
  • The pivot columns of \(A\) itself form the column-space basis; the nonzero rows of \(U\) span the row space. Don't mix the two.
  • \(\dim P_n=n+1\) — the constant term is a dimension.
  • A spanning set that's too big isn't "wrong," it's just dependent: independence is about minimality, spanning about adequacy.

History. The Nine Chapters (~200 AD) already ran elimination on rectangular arrays of coefficients. "matrices" used for 1600 years before anyone named one. Leibniz (1693) and Cramer (1750) played with determinants; Cayley (1858, A Memoir on the Theory of Matrices) was the first to treat the array as an object that you multiply, invert, and equate. "a matrix is not a determinant," he insisted, "but they are closely connected"; Sylvester coined both words. The computational turn is 1930s–40s von Neumann & Turing: a machine, unlike a human, cannot see that a system is nearly singular, so we had to make that perception numerical, hence LU, pivoting strategies, and conditioning/error analysis.

1. Multiplication is composition #

\((AB)_{ij} = (\text{row } i \text{ of } A)\cdot(\text{col } j \text{ of } B)\). The ugly-looking rule has one meaning: \(AB\) is the single matrix that does B's map, then A's. If \(y=Bx\) and \(z=Ay\) then \(z=(AB)x\). That's why products are generally non-commutative: socks-then-shoes ≠ shoes-then-socks. Consequences you'll use constantly: \[ (AB)^T=B^TA^T,\qquad (AB)^{-1}=B^{-1}A^{-1} \] (both reverse order, the "undo in reverse" logic: take off socks before shoes). Column view: column \(j\) of \(AB\) equals \(A\cdot(\text{col } j\text{ of }B)\), a matrix product is just matrix–vector products lined up side by side. That view is also why BLAS parallelizes products so well and why, in §5, \(AP=PD\) is the entire proof of diagonalization in one symbol.

2. Elimination, rank, and LU #

Gaussian elimination = multiplying on the left by elementary matrices that zero entries below pivots: \(E_{31}E_{32}E_{21}A=U\). Undo those operations in the right order and their multipliers reassemble (with flipped signs) into a lower-triangular matrix: \(\boxed{A=LU}\).

Why factor instead of just solving: one \(O(n^3)\) factorization buys infinitely many \(O(n^2)\) solves \(Ly=b,\;Ux=y\), for different right-hand sides. Graphics: one projection factorization, a million vertices per frame. Physics: one stiffness factorization per timestep, many load cases. Control: one system matrix, many inputs. "Factor once, solve many" is the structural idea behind every linear solver you'll ever call, and it's why LU, not Cramer's rule, is what numpy.linalg.solve runs inside.

Solution trichotomy for \(Ax=b\): a pivot ever appears in the augmented column (a row reducing to \(0=5\)) → no solution; no pivot there and no free columns → unique; free columns → infinitely many. The complete-solution shape: \[ x=x_p+c_1n_1+\dots \quad(\text{particular solution} + \text{anything in the null space}) \] This "particular + homogeneous" structure is the exact twin of \(y=y_h+y_p\) for ODEs (§6) and of \(Y=Y_{\text{zero-input}}+Y_{\text{zero-state}}\) (§8), all three are one theorem: solutions of a linear equation form an affine coset of the null space of the linear operator involved.

3. Invertibility: the equivalence chain #

For a square \(A\), these are all equivalent: memorize the whole chain, because exams test the arrows between any two of them: \[ \begin{aligned} \det A\neq0 &\iff A^{-1}\text{ exists} \iff \mathrm{rank}A=n \iff N(A)=\{0\} \\[3pt] &\iff \text{cols independent} \iff \text{cols span } \mathbb{R}^n \iff 0 \text{ is not an eigenvalue} \\[3pt] &\iff Ax=b \text{ has a unique solution for every } b \iff A \text{ is a product of elementary matrices} \end{aligned} \] The 2×2 shortcut (and only for 2×2, it's a favorite wrong-tool temptation on bigger sizes): \[ \begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}^{-1}=\frac1{ad-bc}\begin{bmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{bmatrix} \]

4. Determinants as signed volume #

Definition by properties (the honest way, the axioms are the intuition): a function \(D\) of the rows with \(D(I)=1\); swapping two rows negates \(D\); \(D\) is linear in each row separately. Everything else is derived: two equal rows → \(D=0\); "add a multiple of one row to another" leaves \(D\) unchanged (so elimination preserves it!); triangular → product of the diagonal. That last item is how you actually compute: row-reduce to \(U\), multiply the pivots, times \((-1)^{\#\text{row swaps}}\).

Geometry: \(\det\) is the signed factor by which the map scales \(n\)-dimensional volume. 2×2: area of the image of the unit square (Visual 3 shows this directly); 3×3: the parallelepiped built on the three columns. The sign records orientation (a mirror has negative det). Then \(\det(AB)=\det A\det B\) is obvious, composing two scalings multiplies the factors, and \(\det A=0\) ⇔ volume crushed to zero ⇔ not invertible: all three views are the same fact.

Cofactor expansion \(\det A=\sum_j(-1)^{i+j}a_{ij}M_{ij}\) is the exam tool for 3×3 (expand along the row/column with the most zeros, that's the entire "technique") and \(O(n!)\) in general; nobody computes real determinants that way. The identities \(\det A=\prod\lambda_i\) and \(\mathrm{tr}\,A=\sum\lambda_i\) (§4 proves them) give instant sanity checks: computed three eigenvalues whose sum misses the trace? One of them is wrong.

6. Worked examples + numpy #

Example 1 · solve, invert, and read geometry from one matrix

\(A=\begin{bmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{bmatrix}\): \(\det=4\cdot3-1\cdot2=10\), \(\mathrm{tr}=7\). \(A^{-1}=\frac1{10}\begin{bmatrix}3&-1\\-2&4\end{bmatrix}\), verify one entry: row 1 of A · col 1 of \(A^{-1}\) \(=\frac{12-2}{10}=1\) ✔. The unit square maps to a parallelogram of area 10 (check with Visual 3: columns (4,2) and (1,3) do enclose 10). Contrast \(P=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\): det 0, image = a segment, \(N(P)=\mathrm{span}\{e_2\}\), \(C(P)=\mathrm{span}\{e_1\}\), all four fundamental subspaces visible from memory.

Example 2 · 3×3 elimination, LU, and det: one pass, three prizes

\(A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1&1\\4&3&3\\8&7&9\end{bmatrix}\). \(R_2-2R_1\), \(R_3-4R_1\): \(\begin{bmatrix}2&1&1\\0&1&1\\0&3&5\end{bmatrix}\). Then \(R_3-3R_2\): \(U=\begin{bmatrix}2&1&1\\0&1&1\\0&0&2\end{bmatrix}\). Pivots 2,1,2 ⇒ rank 3, \(\det=2\cdot1\cdot2=4\). The multipliers 2,4,3 go into L (signs already correct since we subtracted them): \(L=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\2&1&0\\4&3&1\end{bmatrix}\). Verify one entry: \((LU)_{32}=4\cdot1+3\cdot1=7\) ✔. Every row-reduction you do by hand this semester is secretly producing an LU, the "wasted" bookkeeping is the factorization.

Example 3 · Cramer's rule: why it's beautiful and why it's dead

For \(A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{bmatrix},\;b=(3,4)\): \(\det A=5\), \(x_1=\frac{\det\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\4&3\end{bmatrix}}{5}=\frac{9-4}{5}=1\), \(x_2=\frac{\det\begin{bmatrix}2&3\\1&4\end{bmatrix}}{5}=\frac{8-3}{5}=1\). Elegant! But Cramer needs \(n+1\) determinants, each \(O(n!)\) by cofactor expansion: for a 10,000×10,000 system, more arithmetic than the sun will ever run. Elimination is \(O(n^3)\) total. Rule of thumb: Cramer for proofs and \(n\le3\); elimination for everything else. (Cramer survives in textbooks because it's a clean formula, and in GPU shaders never at all.)

Example 4 · determinant properties as a computation shortcut

Given \(\det\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}=6\), find \(\det\begin{bmatrix}2a&2b\\c+3a&d+3b\end{bmatrix}\) without ever seeing the entries. "Add 3×row 1 to row 2" doesn't change det; scaling row 1 by 2 doubles it. Answer: 12. This is the entire genre of "det properties" exam questions: reduce to a known matrix using the three rules and keep score.

History. Once Cayley had matrix objects, the geometric reading, rotations, reflections, projections, even differentiation, all as linear maps, unified algebra and geometry. Klein's 1872 Erlangen program went further: a geometry is the set of invariants of a chosen transformation group (Euclidean = invariants of rotations+translations; projective = invariants of a bigger group; "shape" is whatever your group forgets to change). Functional analysis then treated \(d/dt\) and \(\int\) themselves as linear operators on function spaces, the direct bridge to §6–§9, and the reason this chapter sits exactly here. Every "transformation matrix" in a game engine is one of Klein's groups, compiled into 4×4 floats.

1. Definition; kernel, range, rank–nullity #

\(T:V\to W\) is linear \(\iff\) \(T(u+v)=T(u)+T(v)\) and \(T(cv)=cT(v)\). Instant corollaries: \(T(0)=0\) (origin fixed, the fastest disproof for any map that moves it, like translation), and linear maps send lines to lines (or crush them to points): the grid stays parallel and evenly spaced. Visual 3 is literally that sentence.

  • Kernel (null space) \(\ker T=\{v:T(v)=0\}\), everything that collapses. A subspace of \(V\). \(T\) injective \(\iff\ker T=\{0\}\).
  • Image/range \(\mathrm{im}\,T=\{T(v):v\in V\}\), everything reachable. A subspace of \(W\). \(T\) surjective \(\iff \mathrm{im}\,T=W\).
  • Rank–nullity: \(\dim\ker T+\dim\mathrm{im}\,T=\dim V\).
One-paragraph proof worth memorizing (it generalizes everywhere): take a basis of \(\ker T\), extend it to a basis of \(V\). The kernel part maps to 0; the extension vectors have linearly independent images (any dependence would hide another kernel vector in the combination). So \(\dim V\) splits exactly into "dimensions that died" + "dimensions that landed independently." Read again in §6: for an ODE, \(\ker\) = homogeneous solutions, \(\mathrm{im}\) = the functions you can produce by forcing, rank–nullity becomes "solution space dim = order of the equation." Same map, three costumes.

2. The matrix of a transformation #

Fix bases and every linear map \(T:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m\) is a matrix: the matrix has as its \(j\)-th column the image of the \(j\)-th basis vector: \[ [T]=\begin{bmatrix}T(e_1)&\cdots&T(e_n)\end{bmatrix} \] "Linear transformation" and "matrix" are one chapter: the abstract object and its number-table in chosen coordinates. Standard 2-D recipes: \[ \text{Rot}_\theta=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}\quad \text{Ref}_{x}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}\quad \text{Proj}_{x}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\quad \text{Scale}=\begin{bmatrix}s_x&0\\0&s_y\end{bmatrix} \] Derive the rotation yourself (rule of thumb: never accept a formula on authority): where does \(e_1=(1,0)\) go? Straight to \((\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\), column 1. Where does \(e_2=(0,1)\)? The right triangle says \((-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\), column 2. Done. The signs are now yours forever, and you've just performed the single most useful trick of the chapter: read the columns from the geometry.

3. Change of basis and similarity #

Same map, new coordinates. If \(P\) is the matrix whose columns are the new basis vectors (so \(P\) converts new-coordinates → old-coordinates), then the matrix of the same transformation in the new basis is \[ B=P^{-1}AP \qquad(\text{read right-to-left: into P-coords, act with A, back out}) \] and \(A,B\) are similar. Similar matrices share everything coordinate-free: determinant, trace, rank, eigenvalues, characteristic polynomial; they differ only in the number-tuples of the eigenvectors. §5's entire job is: choose \(P\) so that \(B\) is diagonal. This paragraph is the central argument of the whole review, stated plainly, if similarity makes sense to you, diagonalization is just "the most extreme legal change of coordinates," not a trick.

4. Worked examples + numpy #

Example 1 · matrix from images of a NON-standard basis

\(T:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2\) sends \(v_1=(1,1)\mapsto(3,1)\) and \(v_2=(1,-1)\mapsto(1,-3)\), no standard-basis info given. Recover it by linearity: \(e_1=\frac{v_1+v_2}2\Rightarrow T(e_1)=\frac{(3,1)+(1,-3)}2=(2,-1)\); \(e_2=\frac{v_1-v_2}2\Rightarrow T(e_2)=\frac{(3,1)-(1,-3)}2=(1,2)\). So \([T]=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}\), det 5, eigenvalues \(2\pm i\): rotation+stretch by \(\sqrt5\), as §4 will confirm. The "images of a basis" problem is the eigenvector-verification problem run in reverse; one tool, both directions.

Example 2 · differentiation IS a matrix (and a nilpotent one)

\(T:P_3\to P_2,\;T(p)=p'\). Linear: \((cp+dq)'=cp'+dq'\) ✔. \(\ker T=\) constants (dim 1); \(\mathrm{im}\,T=P_2\) (dim 3); rank–nullity: \(1+3=4\) ✔. In the basis \(\{1,x,x^2,x^3\}\): \([T]=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0&0\\0&0&2&0\\0&0&0&3\end{bmatrix}\) (columns = derivatives of basis elements). Differentiation is a 3×4 matrix. Now the sentence that unlocks §6: the eigenfunctions of \(\frac{d}{dt}\) on functions are \(e^{rt}\), because \(\frac{d}{dt}e^{rt}=re^{rt}\) is literally \(Av=\lambda v\) with \(A=\frac{d}{dt}\). The "characteristic equation" of an ODE is the characteristic polynomial of a linear operator, wearing a disguise.

Example 3 · similarity, by hand: diagonalizing before you're told to

Let \(A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\) and use the "45° basis" \(P=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}\) (vectors \((1,1),(1,-1)\)). Then \(P^{-1}=\frac12\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}\) and \[ P^{-1}AP=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix} \] Same map, stretch ×3 along \((1,1)\), ×1 along \((1,-1)\), zero mixing, finally visible. You have diagonalized a matrix before Chapter 5 told you that was allowed. (Those directions are of course its eigenvectors, see you in §4.) Sanity: det \(=3\) both sides ✔, tr \(=4\) both ✔, the invariants.

Example 4 · homogeneous coordinates: making the non-linear linear (§10A's seed)

\(T(x,y)=(x+2,y)\), translation, fails linearity (\(T(0)\neq0\)). But in 3-D with an appended 1: \[ \begin{bmatrix}x'\\y'\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&2\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\\1\end{bmatrix} \] which is linear, the §1 trick in action: extend the space until the troublesome operation becomes linear. Every engine transform is a 4×4 of this form; rotations+translations+perspective compose into one matrix because the extra dimension made the group linear.

demo_transforms.py · build maps from images, compose, and change basis
import numpy as np

th = np.pi/4
R = np.array([[np.cos(th), -np.sin(th)],      # rotation 45°, built the honest way:
              [np.sin(th),  np.cos(th)]])     # col1 = where e1 goes, col2 = e2

print("R@R (90°):", R @ R)                     # composition = multiplication ✔

# homogeneous: rotate 45 then translate (2,0) — one 3x3, no piecewise code

R3 = np.block([[R, np.array([[0.],[0.]])],[[0.,0.,1.]]])

CS uses. Game/graphics engines chain model→view→projection via exactly these matrices; the perspective divide is a projective transform in 4×4 homogeneous form. Color matrices (RGB↔YCbCr, the first operation of every JPEG encoder) are ordinary change-of-basis. PCA projects data onto new axes: §3.3's similarity, applied to a covariance matrix (§4–5). And a neural network layer's pre-activation \(Wx+b\) is a linear map + translation, the nonlinearity is bolted on afterwards precisely because composing only linear maps would collapse to one matrix (do the thought exercise for two layers: \((W_2(W_1x))=(W_2W_1)x\)), the chapter's most practical corollary.
Pitfalls. (1) Translation isn't linear (moves the origin), so it is affine; fix with homogeneous coordinates, never by pretending. (2) Read \(P^{-1}AP\) strictly right-to-left; writing \(APP^{-1}\) is the #1 similarity slip. (3) The same vector has different coordinate lists in different bases: \(x\) vs \(P^{-1}x\). Confusing the vector with its coordinates is the underlying cause of most §5 confusion, the map is the invariant, the matrix is a snapshot.

History. Euler (1740s) found that a rigid body rotates freely about special axes, principal axes, which are eigenvectors, a century before the word existed. Cauchy (1829) proved symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues with orthogonal eigenvectors. Hilbert and Schmidt (early 1900s) carried the theory to infinite dimensions, integral equations and then quantum mechanics, where "eigenvalue" is literally the German-English hybrid physicists kept (eigen = own, characteristic; observables are operators, and what you can measure is their eigenvalues, the most physically loaded math word there is). The computational era: Francis and Kublanovskaya independently found the QR algorithm in 1961, before it, eigenvalues of anything beyond 4×4 were essentially unobtainable by hand or machine; afterwards, "big eigenproblem" became a routine. Brin and Page (1998) spent one on the entire web.

1. Definition, and where the recipe comes from #

\[ Av=\lambda v,\qquad v\neq 0 \]

An eigenvector is a direction the map doesn't bend. It is only scaled by the eigenvalue \(\lambda\). \(\lambda\) may be 0 (the direction is crushed, a null-space direction, "eigen-ness" survives the collapse) or negative (the direction is kept but reversed). \(v=0\) is excluded by definition, else every \(\lambda\) would trivially "work" on it.

The recipe, and its 30-second derivation (so it's never magic): \(Av=\lambda v\iff(\lambda I-A)v=0\). A nonzero solution exists exactly when \(\lambda I-A\) has a nontrivial null space, i.e. is singular, i.e. \(\det(\lambda I-A)=0\) (§2's chain!). That determinant is a degree-\(n\) polynomial in \(\lambda\), the characteristic polynomial, and its roots are the eigenvalues by construction.

\[ \textbf{Step 1: } \det(A-\lambda I)=0 \;\Rightarrow\; \lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n \qquad \textbf{Step 2: for each } \lambda:\; (A-\lambda I)v=0 \text{ by elimination} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{eigenspace basis} \]
For 2×2 there's a one-line shortcut worth deriving once: \(\det(\lambda I-A)=\lambda^2-(\operatorname{tr}A)\lambda+\det A\), the sum of roots is the trace, the product is the determinant. So you can find eigenvalues of any 2×2 from two numbers, and verify in your head. Triangular matrix? The eigenvalues are the diagonal, already visible: the characteristic determinant is triangular too (skip Step 1 entirely).

Step 3 (always): multiply out \(Av\) and confirm it equals \(\lambda v\). Ten seconds; catches nearly every sign slip.

2. Four worked examples #

Ex A · the canonical 2×2 (memorize this computation, cold): A=[[4,1],[2,3]]

\(\det\begin{bmatrix}4-\lambda&1\\2&3-\lambda\end{bmatrix}=(4-\lambda)(3-\lambda)-2=\lambda^2-7\lambda+10=(\lambda-5)(\lambda-2)\), so \(\lambda_1=5,\lambda_2=2\).
\(\lambda=5\): \((A-5I)=\begin{bmatrix}-1&1\\2&-2\end{bmatrix}\to x=y\to v_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}\).   \(\lambda=2\): \(\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\2&1\end{bmatrix}\to y=-2x\to v_2=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\end{bmatrix}\).
Check: \(A(1,1)^T=(5,5)=5(1,1)\) ✔, \(A(1,-2)^T=(2,-4)=2(1,-2)\) ✔. Sanity: \(5+2=7=\operatorname{tr}\) ✔, \(5\cdot2=10=\det\) ✔. Load "Eigen Ex A" in Visual 3 and the two dashed lines are these vectors, the algebra and the arrow agree, which is the point of both.

Ex B · a rotation has NO real eigenvalues: R=[[0,−1],[1,0]]

\(\lambda^2+1=0\Rightarrow\lambda=\pm i\). Geometrically instant: a 90° turn moves every nonzero vector off its own line, so no real eigenvector can exist. "No real eigenvalues" is a correct, examinable answer, and not a dead end: a complex pair \(\alpha\pm i\beta\) means rotation combined with scaling, because over \(\mathbb{C}\), \(e^{i\beta t}\) is a rotation by Euler's formula. §7's spirals (real trajectories that orbit while growing/decaying) and §8's complex poles (ringing in circuits) are this exact fact re-skinned. Also: \(\det=1, \operatorname{tr}=0\Rightarrow\lambda=\pm i\) from the trace/det shortcut without any determinant.

Ex C · defective, non-diagonalizable: J=[[1,1],[0,1]]

\((\lambda-1)^2=0\): one eigenvalue with algebraic multiplicity 2. \((J-I)=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\Rightarrow y=0\): the eigenspace is \(\mathrm{span}\{(1,0)\}\), geometric multiplicity 1 < 2. One eigenvector only, so \(J\) cannot be diagonalized (§5). The geometry: J is identity + shear. It stretches nothing along either axis and bends along none; it slides the plane, so there's genuinely no second special direction to find. The defective test to memorize: for every \(\lambda\), does \(\operatorname{rank}(A-\lambda I)=n-(\text{algebraic mult.})\) fail? Here \(\operatorname{rank}(J-I)=1\) but needs to be 0. That's the whole diagnostic in a rank computation.

Ex D · a 3×3 where you should compute nothing: triangular

\(A=\begin{bmatrix}2&5&9\\0&3&7\\0&0&-1\end{bmatrix}\) is upper-triangular ⇒ \(\lambda=2,3,-1\), read, don't grind. Trace \(=4=\) sum ✔; \(\det=-6=\) product ✔. Look for structure before reaching for the cubic: exams hide triangles in 3×3s constantly (a column of zeros = you can expand the determinant immediately; two zeros in a row = cofactor along it).

5. Visual 5 · power iteration (eigenvalues in motion) #

The algorithm that ranks the web is three lines: start with any random \(x\); repeatedly set \(x\leftarrow Ax/\|Ax\|\). Why it works is a one-line proof: expand \(x_0\) in the eigenbasis, then \[ A^kx_0=\lambda_1^k\Big(c_1v_1+\sum_{i\ge2}c_i(\lambda_i/\lambda_1)^k v_i\Big)\longrightarrow \lambda_1^kc_1v_1 \] every non-dominant component shrinks geometrically at rate \(|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|^k\). It's slow but embarrassingly simple, and it scales to matrices with billions of rows because it only ever needs products, never a determinant. (This is exactly §5's \(A^k=PD^kP^{-1}\) behavior, watched from the inside.)

Visual 5 · Power iteration sandbox The running vector \(x\) (red) chasing the dominant eigenvector (dashed), on the same matrix as Visual 4.
Ex A → x locks onto the λ=5 direction within ~6 steps: \(|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|=0.4\), so the error divides by ~2.5 per step: you can watch the geometric rate. Rotation → x circles forever and never converges: both eigenvalues have the same modulus, so there's no "dominant" direction. Conclusion for the exam: convergence is about gaps between eigenvalue moduli, not sizes. That's why PageRank's damping factor exists (and why Krylov/shift-invert methods are built the way they are): they manufacture gaps. Changing a–d here also refreshes Visual 4 above, so the two always agree.

6. PageRank, end-to-end #

A link is a vote. Put page \(j\)'s out-links in column \(j\): split 1 unit among them → a column-stochastic matrix \(M\) (columns sum to 1). Importance \(x\) should satisfy \(x=Mx\), i.e. be the eigenvector for \(\lambda=1\). Existence is guaranteed (Perron–Frobenius: a nonnegative matrix with column sums 1 has \(\lambda_1=1\) with a nonnegative eigenvector), and it's found by power iteration, the only reason the algorithm scales.

\[ M=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\tfrac12\\1&0&\tfrac12\\0&1&0\end{bmatrix},\quad x_{k+1}=Mx_k,\quad x_0=\big(\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13\big)\ \longrightarrow\ x_\infty=\big(\tfrac15,\tfrac25,\tfrac25\big) \]

Pages 1→2→3→1, plus 3→2 (page 3 splits its vote). Solve the fixed point by hand instead of trusting the machine: \(x_1=\tfrac12x_3,\;x_2=x_1+\tfrac12x_3,\;x_3=x_2,\;x_1+x_2+x_3=1\) ⇒ \((1,2,2)/5\). Where eigenvalues live in CS. Markov chains: the stationary distribution is \(\lambda=1\)'s eigenvector, and convergence speed is \(|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|^k\) (PageRank, MCMC, Google's original patent). PCA: principal axes = eigenvectors of the covariance matrix (symmetric ⇒ real + orthogonal, the spectral theorem earns its keep). Spectral graph theory: the Laplacian \(L=D-A\)'s second-smallest eigenvalue (the "algebraic connectivity," Fiedler vector) decides good graph cuts, clustering, partitioning, VLSI layout. Recurrences: Fibonacci above. Stability: \(\operatorname{Re}\lambda<0\) ⇒ decay, the whole of §7. Search/ranking: latent semantic indexing is eigen/SVD on a term-document matrix. Pitfalls. (1) \(v=0\) never, \(\lambda=0\) fine (and means "singular". §2's chain). (2) Eigenvectors are unique only up to scale: a solutions key's \((1,1)\) and your \((2,2)\) and numpy's \((0.707,0.707)\) are the same eigenvector, compare ratios, never digits. (3) Repeated eigenvalue ≠ repeated eigenvectors: check the dimension of the eigenspace, not the algebraic multiplicity (Ex C). (4) A non-symmetric real matrix can have complex eigenvalues, not an error, a diagnosis (rotation, §7). (5) \(A\) and \(A^T\) share \(\lambda\) but their eigenvectors are different families; for symmetric matrices they coincide. That is not a trivial special case; it is the spectral theorem.

1. Diagonalization #

\[ A=PDP^{-1},\quad P=\begin{bmatrix}v_1&\cdots&v_n\end{bmatrix},\ D=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)\quad\Rightarrow\quad A^k=PD^kP^{-1},\ e^{At}=Pe^{Dt}P^{-1} \]

Why it works, in one line: the eigen-relations \(Av_i=\lambda_iv_i\) for all \(i\) are exactly the matrix equation \(AP=PD\) (columns on both sides), and if \(P\) is invertible (n independent eigenvectors, there's the condition) then \(A=PDP^{-1}\). No trickery, just restating §4 with block notation.

When it works: \(n\) independent eigenvectors exist \(\iff\) geometric = algebraic multiplicity for every \(\lambda\). Two green lights that cover 95% of exam cases: distinct eigenvalues (then the eigenvectors are automatically independent) and real symmetric (spectral theorem gives it for free, and even with an orthogonal P: \(A=PDP^T\), so \(A^k=PD^kP^T\) needs no inverse).

What it buys, three things, all of them appear later in these pages:

  1. Powers: \(A^k=PD^kP^{-1}\), raise n scalars instead of multiplying n times. Markov chains and Fibonacci (§4) live here.
  2. Matrix exponentials: \(e^{At}=Pe^{Dt}P^{-1}=P\,\mathrm{diag}(e^{\lambda_it})P^{-1}\), which is literally §7's solution formula \(x=e^{At}x_0\) unpacked.
  3. Decoupling: substitute \(x=Py\) into \(x'=Ax\): then \(y'=Dy\), n independent scalar equations, solved instantly, then rotated back. "Change to the eigenbasis so nothing mixes" is §0's thesis cashed out. (Do this substitution yourself once; it's three lines and it removes all mystery from §7.)
Worked: A¹⁰ of §4's Ex A, four steps

\(P=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&-2\end{bmatrix}\Rightarrow\det P=-3\neq0\) (invertibility check, do it explicitly every time), \(P^{-1}=\frac1{-3}\begin{bmatrix}-2&-1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix}\). \(D^{10}=\mathrm{diag}(5^{10},2^{10})=\mathrm{diag}(9\,765\,625,1024)\). Multiply out: \[ A^{10}=P D^{10}P^{-1}=\tfrac13\begin{bmatrix}2\cdot5^{10}+2^{10}&5^{10}-2^{10}\\2(5^{10}-2^{10})&5^{10}+2\cdot2^{10}\end{bmatrix}=\tfrac13\begin{bmatrix}19\,532\,274&9\,764\,601\\19\,529\,202&9\,767\,673\end{bmatrix} \] Two exponentiations + two matrix multiplications, versus nine 2×2 products. And the structure of the answer tells a story: every entry is dominated by \(5^{10}\), the largest eigenvalue raised to the power, the direction \(v_1\) wins, which is power iteration (Visual 5) in closed form. Both pictures are the same fact.

2. When it fails: defective matrices and Jordan #

§4 Ex C's \(J=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\) has one eigenvector, no basis exists in which it's diagonal. The next-best normal form is the Jordan form: eigenvalues on the diagonal, 1s on the superdiagonal, with generalized eigenvectors \((A-\lambda I)v=w\neq0)\) bridging the missing directions. For a review at this level you need only this much: it always exists, diagonalization is the special case where every Jordan block is 1×1, and the tell-tale symptom is \(te^{\lambda t}\) terms, in §6 that's the "repeated characteristic root" case, in §7 it's the degenerate node. Same defect, three costumes. Bonus tool that dodges Jordan entirely on small problems: Cayley–Hamilton, the characteristic polynomial kills the matrix, \(p(A)=0\), so for 2×2, \(A^2-(\operatorname{tr}A)A+(\det A)I=0\) gives \(A^{-1}=\frac1{\det A}(\operatorname{tr}A\cdot I-A)\) in one line, and \(A^n\) as a linear combination of \(I,A\).

3. The SVD, derived #

Diagonalization demands square and enough eigenvectors. The Singular Value Decomposition demands nothing:

\[ A_{m\times n}=U_{m\times m}\,\Sigma_{m\times n}\,V_n^T,\qquad \sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\cdots\ge0,\quad U^TU=I,\ V^TV=I \]

The derivation is the intuition, so here it is rather than declared. Ask the geometric question §0's Visual 3 already posed: which unit direction does A stretch the most? That's maximizing \(\|Av\|^2=v^TA^Tv\) over \(\|v\|=1\), a quadratic form, whose extremum is the top eigenvector of \(A^TA\) (Lagrange multipliers give \(A^TAv=\sigma^2v\) directly). And \(A^TA\) is always symmetric with nonnegative eigenvalues, so the spectral theorem hands us a real orthonormal eigenbasis \(V\) for free. Call the maxima \(u_i=Av_i/\sigma_i\) (with \(\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i(A^TA)}\)), extend to orthonormal bases, and by construction \(AV=U\Sigma\), i.e. \(A=U\Sigma V^T\). Nothing about being square or diagonalizable was used.

Read it as §3's geometry (this is the sentence to say out loud while dragging Visual 6): \(V^T\) rotates the input space into the singular-vector frame, \(\Sigma\) stretches along the coordinate axes by \(\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\dots\), and \(U\) rotates into the output frame. Every matrix is rotate → stretch → rotate. Consequences that will recur: the unit circle always lands on an ellipse (the "image of the unit sphere"); \(\sigma_1\) is the most any unit vector is stretched (the operator norm), \(\sigma_{\min}\) the least; rank = number of nonzero \(\sigma\); \(\kappa=\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}\) is the conditioning number of §2's "nearly singular," measured not guessed.

Gold/rose arrows = right singular vectors \(v_1,v_2\) (orthogonal in the domain); green/purple = \(\sigma_1u_1,\sigma_2u_2\) (the ellipse's own axes, orthogonal in the range).
Guided tour. Pause at stage 0.5: the circle has rotated but not stretched. \(V^T\) alone. At 1.5 the ellipse is axis-aligned; the final half-stage only tilts it: that's \(U\). Symmetric: the ellipse's axes coincide with the eigen-directions from Visual 3. SVD collapses to eigen-decomposition (up to signs absorbing negative eigenvalues). Rotation: σ₁=σ₂=1, nothing stretched; a rotation is "all rotate, no stretch," which is what orthogonal matrices are. Projection: σ₂=0, one axis annihilated; rank visibly = number of nonzero σ. Diagonal: V and U are already identity, a diagonal matrix is its own SVD, which is the whole point of §5.1 in miniature: diagonal = simple, and SVD is the procedure that finds the coordinates where any matrix is as close to diagonal as possible.

5. Low-rank approximation #

Eckart–Young–Mirsky theorem: among all matrices of rank ≤ k, the closest to \(A\) (in both Frobenius and spectral norms) is exactly the truncated SVD \[ A_k=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_iu_iv_i^{T}=U_k\Sigma_kV_k^{T},\qquad \|A-A_k\|_2=\sigma_{k+1} \]

The best rank-k approximation is the first k SVD terms, and the error you eat is literally the first singular value you threw away. That single sentence is PCA, latent semantic indexing, JPEG's DCT cousin, and the Netflix Prize (§10D): real-world data has fast-decaying \(\sigma_i\), so k small terms reproduce it almost exactly while storing \(k(m+n+1)\) numbers instead of \(mn\).

demo_svd.py · rebuild, then compress an image with a rank-k truncated SVD
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[3.,0.],[4.,5.]])
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A)
print("sigma      =", s)                       # [6.708 2.236] = 3√5, √5 — the §5 Ex2 answer
print("rebuild err =", np.abs(U@np.diag(s)@Vt - A).max())  # ~1e-15 ✔
print("stretch ratio (kappa) =", s[0]/s[-1])     # the conditioning number, measured

# The compression demo: a smooth gradient + noise, stored at rank k
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X = np.tile(np.linspace(0,1,100), (100,1)) + rng.normal(0,0.08,(100,100))
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X)
print("first 6 sigmas:", np.round(s[:6],2))      # look how fast they drop — that's
# "this image has structure." Random noise alone would be a flat sigma spectrum.
for k in (1,2,5,10,100):
    err = np.norm(X - U[:,:k]@np.diag(s[:k])@Vt[:k]) / np.norm(X)
    print(f"rank {k:>3}:  rel. error {err:.3f}   stores {k*(2*100+1)} of 10000 numbers")
# rank 2: ~0.10 error from 402 numbers (25x smaller). This is the Netflix trick
# and it is literally "sum of the first k sigma_i u_i v_i^T" — §10D.

# PCA in 6 lines, because it IS the SVD of the centered data
Xd = rng.normal(0,1,(500,2)) @ np.array([[3.,0.],[0.,0.3]])   # anisotropic cloud
print((Xd - Xd.mean(0)).T @ (Xd - Xd.mean(0)))   # covariance (up to 1/n): symmetric —
# spectral theorem applies: real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors = principal axes.
Uses. PCA, principal components are the columns of \(V\), and "variance explained" is \(\sigma_i^2/\sum_j\sigma_j^2\) (that pie chart in every notebook is a singular-value spectrum). Recommenders (§10D). Rank & the pseudoinverse: \(A^+=V\Sigma^+U^T\) inverts the nonzero σ's and ignores the crushed directions, the least-squares solution every regression library returns, including underdetermined/overdetermined cases where "the" inverse doesn't exist. Numerics: \(\kappa(A)=\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}\) predicts how many digits of accuracy your solve loses (§2's condition demo). NLP: LSI = SVD on the term-document matrix. Computer vision: essential matrix decomposition, structure from motion. Signal: total least squares.
Pitfalls. (1) SVD of a symmetric matrix is not its eigen-decomposition: same directions up to signs, but singular values are \(|\lambda|\), a negative eigenvalue becomes a positive σ with a flipped column of U. (2) \(A^k=PD^kP^{-1}\) requires \(P\) from independent eigenvectors, check \(\det P\neq0\) explicitly; if the columns are secretly dependent, the formula "computes" garbage that looks plausible. (3) In §7 the exponential \(e^{At}=Pe^{Dt}P^{-1}\): the entries of \(P\) don't get exponentiated, only \(D\)'s, a classic misread. (4) Order matters: \(U\Sigma V^T\) with mismatched orderings gives a wrong matrix silently (numpy sorts σ descending; keep the pairing consistent if you truncate by hand).

History. Newton (1670s) wrote differential equations to describe motion. ODEs were the reason calculus was invented, not a later application. The Bernoullis (1690s) built the first-order toolkit (the brachistochrone was the poster child); Euler gave us the constant-coefficient method and the integrating factor; Lagrange, variation of parameters. Picard and Lindelöf (1890s) finally answered "does a solution even exist, and is it unique?" rigorously, the theorem that guarantees the curves in Visual 7 never cross. Most models a CS student meets are first- or second-order with constant coefficients: about 10% of ODE theory, covering 90% of engineering.

1. First-order: recognition, then routine #

The skill being tested is recognition, match the equation to a type, then the method is mechanical:

TypeSignatureMethod, in one sentence
Separable\(y'=g(x)h(y)\)\(\int dy/h(y)=\int g(x)\,dx\), get all y left, all x right
Linear\(y'+P(x)y=Q(x)\)Integrating factor \(\mu=e^{\int P\,dx}\); then \((\mu y)'=\mu Q\), integrate both sides
Exact\(M\,dx+N\,dy=0\) with \(M_y=N_x\)Find a potential \(\psi\) with \(\psi_x=M,\psi_y=N\); the solutions are the level curves \(\psi=C\)
Bernoulli\(y'+Py=Qy^{n}\)Substitute \(u=y^{1-n}\), the nonlinearity exponent becomes 0: it's linear again
Homogeneous\(y'=F(y/x)\)Substitute \(v=y/x\); it becomes separable

Derive the integrating factor so it's not a spell. You want to multiply \(y'+Py=Q\) by some \(\mu\) making the left side an exact derivative: \(\mu y'+\mu Py\stackrel{!}{=}(\mu y)'=\mu y'+\mu'y\), matching middle terms gives \(\mu'/\mu=P\Rightarrow\mu=e^{\int P}\). Every "trick" on this table has such a two-line derivation; learn the derivation once and the table has no arbitrary rows. (Exact equations are the same logic run backwards: \(M_y=N_x\) is precisely the integrability condition \(\psi_{xy}=\psi_{yx}\).)

Why exactly 2 arbitrary constants for 2nd order? §1.1 answered it in advance: the solutions of a linear homogeneous ODE form a vector space whose dimension = the order, and initial conditions \((y(0),y'(0))\) are coordinates on it (the map \(y\mapsto(y(0),y'(0))\) is an isomorphism). "General solution \(=C_1y_1+C_2y_2\)" is not notation. It's a statement that \(\{y_1,y_2\}\) is a basis of the solution space. §7 makes this literal by turning the ODE into a 2×2 system.

2. Second-order, constant coefficients #

\[ ay''+by'+cy=0\ \Longrightarrow\ ar^2+br+c=0\ \Longrightarrow\ y=\begin{cases} C_1e^{r_1t}+C_2e^{r_2t} & r_1\neq r_2\in\mathbb{R}\quad(\text{overdamped / node})\\[2pt] (C_1+C_2t)e^{rt} & r \text{ repeated}\quad(\text{critical / degenerate})\\[2pt] e^{\alpha t}\big(C_1\cos\beta t+C_2\sin\beta t\big) & r=\alpha\pm i\beta\quad(\text{underdamped / spiral})\end{cases} \]

Why \(e^{rt}\) is the right guess, and it's §4: test \(y=e^{rt}\): then \(y'=re^{rt}\) and \(y''=r^2e^{rt}\), so substituting gives \((ar^2+br+c)e^{rt}=0\), and \(e^{rt}\neq0\) so the quadratic must vanish. Read it in operator language: \(e^{rt}\) is an eigenfunction of \(\frac{d}{dt}\) (\(\frac{d}{dt}e^{rt}=re^{rt}\), §3 Ex 2), so the "characteristic equation" is the characteristic equation of the operator \(aD^2+bD+c\). The repeated-root case is the ODE shadow of §4 Ex C's defective matrix, when the operator has only one exponential solution, the rescue vector is the generalized one, and \(te^{rt}\) is a generalized eigenfunction: differentiate \(te^{rt}\) and you get \(e^{rt}+rte^{rt}\): the operator "almost" kills it, exactly the Jordan-chain behavior \( (A-\lambda I)w=v\). The complex case unfolds via Euler, \(e^{(\alpha+i\beta)t}=e^{\alpha t}(\cos\beta t+i\sin\beta t)\), and taking real/imaginary parts gives the two independent real solutions.

Translate the three cases once, remember them forever (this IS §7's phase portrait in words): real distinct roots, sum of two exponentials: over/under-shooting approach to equilibrium, no oscillation; complex \(\alpha\pm i\beta\), sinusoid in a \(e^{\alpha t}\) envelope: ring then settle (α<0) or ring then blow up (α>0); repeated, the fast-without-overshoot knife-edge. A mass-spring-damper is literally this equation (m=1, b=c/damping, c=k/stiffness), so the classification you'll do abstractly in §7 has your hands already felt it in a physics course.

3. Forcing terms: undetermined coefficients & resonance #

\(ay''+by'+cy=g(t)\): general solution = \(y_h+y_p\). Undetermined coefficients, guess \(y_p\) from the same family as \(g\): \(e^{at}\to Ae^{at}\); \(\sin/\cos\to A\cos\omega t+B\sin\omega t\); polynomial of degree n → polynomial of degree n; products combine guesses. Then perform the exam's most-forgotten check: does your guess overlap \(y_h\)? If yes, multiply by \(t\) (by \(t^2\) if the overlap was a repeated root). Overlap = resonance: drive a system at its natural frequency and the response grows like \(t\cdot\)(oscillation), unbounded amplitude. Variation of parameters (\(y_p=-y_1\!\int\!\frac{y_2g}{W}+y_2\!\int\!\frac{y_1g}{W}\), \(W\) the Wronskian) handles any \(g\) at all, heavier, universal; it's also, in §8's language, the convolution formula \(y_p=g*h\) where \(h\) is the impulse response. The two chapters are one idea.

4. Visual 7 · slope fields: the solution family, unsolved #

Visual 7 · Slope field + RK4 sample curves At every \((x,y)\) the field draws a short segment of slope \(y'\). Each colored curve is a different constant \(C\), generated by RK4 stepping along the field, which is exactly what numerical solvers do.
Read the logistic field (y(1−y)) carefully: y≡0 and y≡1 are horizontal lines, equilibrium solutions (\(y'=0\)); below 0 the flow falls away, between 0 and 1 it rises to 1, above 1 it falls back to 1. You just did §7's stability classification on a scalar ODE by looking: 0 is unstable, 1 is stable, before solving anything. Curves cannot cross (uniqueness): two solutions touching would share an initial condition. This picture is the qualitative-theory seed Poincaré grew into phase portraits.

5. Worked examples #

Ex 1 · linear first-order, integrating factor derived and used: y′+2y=e⁻ᵗ, y(0)=1 (you'll redo this exact problem with Laplace in §8; watch the two tools agree)

\(\mu=e^{2t}\): \((e^{2t}y)'=e^{t}\Rightarrow e^{2t}y=e^{t}+C\Rightarrow y=e^{-t}+Ce^{-2t}\). Apply \(y(0)=1\): \(1+C=1\Rightarrow C=0\): \(y=e^{-t}\). Verify by substitution: \(y'+2y=-e^{-t}+2e^{-t}=e^{-t}\) ✔. Structure readout: the general solution is \(y_p=e^{-t}\) (the forcing's family) plus \(Ce^{-2t}\) (the homogeneous memory). The \(e^{-2t}\) term dies at rate 2, the pole at \(s=-2\) §8 will draw, the eigenvalue of the 1×1 system \(x'=-2x\) §7 will classify.

Ex 2 · the standard 2nd-order IVP: y″−3y′+2y=0, y(0)=1, y′(0)=0

\(r^2-3r+2=(r-1)(r-2)=0\Rightarrow r=1,2\). \(y=C_1e^{t}+C_2e^{2t}\). Two conditions, two unknowns (§1's "coordinates"): \(y(0)=C_1+C_2=1\); \(y'(0)=C_1+2C_2=0\). Subtract: \(C_2=-1,\ C_1=2\). \(y=2e^{t}-e^{2t}\). Sanity: \(y(0)=1\) ✔, \(y'(0)=2-2=0\) ✔. Note the answer contains a growing term. That is not a mistake but the diagnosis \(r=1>0\): this IVP diverges, which §7's unstable-node row predicts (both eigenvalues positive). Applying ICs is literally solving a 2×2 linear system from §2, the matrix is \(\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\r_1&r_2\end{bmatrix}\) (a Vandermonde; invertible iff \(r_1\neq r_2\), which is why distinct roots give a basis and the repeated root needs the \(t\)-rescue).

Ex 3 · repeated root: y″+4y′+4y=0

\((r+2)^2=0\): one root, \(r=-2\). First solution \(e^{-2t}\); the defective-root rule (multiply by \(t\)) gives the second: \(y=(C_1+C_2t)e^{-2t}\). Verify \(y=te^{-2t}\): \(y'=e^{-2t}(1-2t)\), \(y''=e^{-2t}(-4+4t)\); plug: \(e^{-2t}[(-4+4t)+4(1-2t)+4t]=0\) ✔. Visual 10 at ζ=1 is this solution, the critically damped curve, and §7's degenerate node is the same object seen as a system.

Ex 4 · resonance: y″+4y=sin2t

\(y_h=C_1\cos2t+C_2\sin2t\). The forcing \(\sin2t\) is a homogeneous term, overlap detected before guessing (the exam's favorite trap): try \(y_p=t(A\cos2t+B\sin2t)\). Compute: \(y_p'=A\cos2t+B\sin2t+2t(-A\sin2t+B\cos2t)\); \(y_p''=2(-A\sin2t+B\cos2t)+2(-A\sin2t+B\cos2t)+4t(-A\cos2t-B\sin2t)=4(-A\sin2t+B\cos2t)-4y_p\). So \(y_p''+4y_p=4(-A\sin2t+B\cos2t)=\sin2t\) ⇒ \(A=-\tfrac14,\ B=0\): \[ y_p=-\tfrac{t}{4}\cos2t \] Amplitude grows linearly forever: energy pumped in at the exact resonance frequency never leaves. This is Tacoma Narrows, the wine glass, and "why your simulation exploded when the timestep matched the mode," all in one formula. Moral: write \(y_h\) first, compare, then guess.

Ex 5 · separable with a hidden constant-loss: Newton's cooling dT/dt=−k(T−T_env)

Let \(u=T-T_{env}\): \(u'=-ku\) separates to \(\ln|u|=-kt+C\Rightarrow u=Ce^{-kt}\Rightarrow T=T_{env}+(T_0-T_{env})e^{-kt}\). Two structural lessons: (1) the equilibrium \(T=T_{env}\) reappears as \(u=0\), separable equations often hide equilibria in the \(h(y)=0\) division you performed; if you divided by \(h\), check those lost solutions (here: exactly the steady state). (2) Exponential approach at rate k, the pattern of Visual 10's decay envelope and the \(e^{-at}\) of Visual 11: every linear first-order system is this equation in a disguise.

Ex 6 · exact equation: (2x+y)dx+(x+2y)dy=0

Test: \(M_y=1=N_x\) ✔ exact. Integrate \(M\) in x: \(\psi=x^2+xy+f(y)\); match \(\psi_y=x+2y=N\Rightarrow f(y)=y^2\). Solutions: \(x^2+xy+y^2=C\), ellipses. Notice (preview §5's quadratic-form application): the form \(x^TAx\) with symmetric \(A=\begin{bmatrix}1&\tfrac12\\\tfrac12&1\end{bmatrix}\) has positive eigenvalues ⇒ ellipses not hyperbolas, and the ellipse axes are the eigenvector directions \((1,1),(1,-1)\) (the §3 Ex 3 change of basis!). The phase portrait of the system this ODE defines is a center, because \(\operatorname{tr}=0\). Three chapters, one example.

# --- RK4 from scratch: the integrator inside solve_ivp, scipy, and game engines ---
def rk4(f, y0, t0, h, n):
    y = float(y0)
    for i in range(n):
        t = t0 + i*h
        k1 = f(t, y)
        k2 = f(t + h/2, y + h/2*k1)
        k3 = f(t + h/2, y + h/2*k2)
        k4 = f(t + h,    y + h*k3)
        y += h*(k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)/6
    return y

f = lambda t, y: -2*y + np.exp(-t)     # Ex 1's equation
print(rk4(f, 1.0, 0.0, 0.01, 100), np.exp(-1))   # 0.3678794... both — RK4 nails it

# --- Stiffness demo (the reason "explicit Euler blows up" is an exam fact) ---
def euler(lam, h, n, y0=1.0):
    y = y0
    for _ in range(n): y = y + h*lam*y
    return y

print("Euler on y'=-2y, h=0.4:", euler(-2, .4, 20))  # decays cleanly: mult=+0.2, no sign flip

History. Poincaré (1880s) founded qualitative dynamics: determine what solutions do, settle, spiral, diverge, from a handful of computed numbers, without ever solving. Lyapunov (1890s) made stability rigorous. This machinery is why control theory, robotics, and epidemiology work: you usually cannot write the trajectory, but you can compute five numbers and know whether the system recovers. In CS terms: phase portraits are the mathematics of "does it settle?", the question behind every simulation, controller, and load-balancing loop you'll ever trust.

1. Solving x′ = Ax #

Try \(x=ve^{rt}\): \(x'=rv e^{rt}\), \(Ax=ve^{rt}r\), substituting gives \(Av=rv\). Each eigenpair is a solution \(e^{\lambda t}v\), and the solution space is a vector space (§1/§6's remark), so linear combinations are solutions:

\[ x(t)=c_1e^{\lambda_1t}v_1+c_2e^{\lambda_2t}v_2 \qquad(\text{distinct real }\lambda\text{'s; apply }x(0)\text{ to find }c_1,c_2) \]

The three cases, which are §6.2 verbatim (a second-order scalar ODE is a 2×2 system, see Ex 2): complex pair \(\alpha\pm i\beta\) with eigenvector \(a+ib\) unfolds by Euler into the two real solutions \(e^{\alpha t}(a\cos\beta t-b\sin\beta t)\) and \(e^{\alpha t}(a\sin\beta t+b\cos\beta t)\), spirals; repeated defective root gives \((v,\ tv+u)e^{\lambda t}\) where \((A-\lambda I)u=v\), the Jordan rescue, again. The coordinate-free one-liner is \(x(t)=e^{At}x_0\) with \(e^{At}=Pe^{Dt}P^{-1}\) (§5's item 2), the decoupling substitution \(x=Py\) makes the proof two lines.

The physical reading, which makes every classification obvious: each eigenmode is an independent "channel" of the system: \(v_i\) is a shape the system can move in, \(e^{\lambda_it}\) is that shape's time profile. Decay/grow/rotate is decided by \(\operatorname{Re}\lambda_i\) alone; shapes never mix (they're the coordinates where nothing mixes. §0's thesis). Nonlinear system near an equilibrium? Linearize: \(x'=Jx\) with \(J\) the Jacobian (§2's CS use) and everything on this page applies approximately. Hartman–Grobman's promise, which you'll take on faith here and which is why "eigenvalues of the Jacobian" is the answer to most of engineering.

2. Classification with only tr and det #

For \(A=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}\): \(\lambda^2-(\mathrm{tr})\lambda+\det=0\): two numbers decide everything:

The rule to internalize: stability ⇔ \(\mathrm{tr}<0\) and \(\det>0\) (for 2×2), no eigenvalue computation needed; that's the whole content of "all Re λ < 0."

4. Worked example #

Full IVP: x′=[[1,1],[4,1]]·x, x(0)=(2,0) · compute, classify, interpret

Classify first (§7.2's point): tr=2, det=1−4=−3<0 → saddle, unstable, before any eigenvector is found. Then solve: \(\lambda^2-2\lambda-3=0\Rightarrow\lambda=3,-1\) (opposite signs ✔ consistent with the saddle). \(\lambda=3\): \((A-3I)=\begin{bmatrix}-2&1\\4&-2\end{bmatrix}\Rightarrow v_1=(1,2)\). \(\lambda=-1\): \(\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\4&2\end{bmatrix}\Rightarrow v_2=(1,-2)\). \[ x(t)=c_1e^{3t}\binom12+c_2e^{-t}\binom1{-2},\qquad c_1+c_2=2,\;2c_1-2c_2=0\Rightarrow c_1=c_2=1 \] Interpret (this is the exam's part b): \(x(t)=e^{3t}(1,2)+e^{-t}(1,-2)\). The \(e^{-t}\) mode dies; the solution eventually runs along the λ=3 eigen-direction, unless you start exactly on the stable line \((1,-2)\), where the coefficient vanishes and you decay to 0. A saddle is stable on a set of measure zero. That's the whole philosophy of stabilization: almost every perturbation eventually aligns you with the dominant mode.

Scalar-to-system conversion, and the SIR case study (the model with a threshold hiding in its eigenvalues)

Any \(n\)-th-order ODE becomes an \(n\times n\) system by stacking derivatives: \(x_1=y,\ x_2=y'\): \(y''+2y'+y=0\Rightarrow x'=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-1&-2\end{bmatrix}x\): tr=−2, det=1, disc=0 → repeated λ=−1, the same critical damping as §6 Ex 3, seen as a system. This is literally how solve_ivp sees your ODE.

SIR epidemic (\(S'= -\beta SI,\ I'=\beta SI-\gamma I\)): the disease-free equilibrium \((S,I)=(1,0)\) has Jacobian \(\begin{bmatrix}-\beta&-\beta\\0&\beta-\gamma\end{bmatrix}\), triangular, so read it (Ex D's skill): \(\lambda=\beta-\gamma,\ \lambda=0\) (the \(\lambda=0\) direction is the disease-free state itself, which is why this Jacobian is always singular); the infected-exponential \(I'\approx(\beta-\gamma)I\) grows iff \(\beta/\gamma>1\). R₀ = β/γ is an eigenvalue ratio. "herd immunity threshold" is a stability statement in disguise, and it's the same \(|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|\)-style thinking as Visual 5.

Uses. Everything that settles, rings, or diverges: RLC transients; robot equilibria ("can this posture be stabilized?"); power-grid small-signal stability (an \(A\) with 10⁴ states. QR algorithm §4's raison d'être); load-balancing and consensus loops in distributed systems (eigenvalues of the mixing matrix = convergence rate of every gossip algorithm); flocking/steering; page-rank-adjacent random walks as continuous-time chains. The skill the industry pays for is exactly this section's: predict behavior without simulating it.
Pitfalls. (1) center vs. stable spiral is the trace sign, and it's delicate, near-tr = 0 systems are hard to classify from approximate matrices (and nonlinear terms decide, know the caveat). (2) Eigenvector scaling is free, pick clean numbers; \(c_i\) absorbs it. (3) For complex λ write the real combination or your "solution" is complex-valued for a real system (the Euler expansion of §7.1, usually the missing step). (4) The \(c_i\) come from solving at \(t=0\), a system of equations for the coefficients in the eigenbasis: convert \(x(0)\) with \(P^{-1}\), don't eyeball it. (5) \(x'=Ax+f\): the homogeneous story classifies the transient; \(f\) adds a particular/forced response (§6's \(y_h+y_p\) again, and §8's tool handles the whole thing in one transform).

History. Laplace (1780s) met this integral in probability and celestial mechanics, solving ODEs was a side effect. Heaviside (1890s) turned it into engineers' "operational calculus," treating \(p=d/dt\) as an algebraic symbol to crack circuit equations by pure manipulation. It worked brilliantly and was attacked for decades as unrigorous; the foundations (Doetsch, Widder, and later Schwartz's distributions to legitimize \(\delta\)) arrived only in the 1930s–50s. Once transfer functions matured in 20th-century control theory, Laplace became its working language: the \(s\)-domain is where engineers actually think.

1. The transform, de-magicked #

\[ F(s)=\mathcal{L}\{f(t)\}=\int_0^\infty e^{-st}f(t)\,dt,\qquad s=\sigma+i\omega \]

What it secretly is: the (one-sided) inner product of \(f\) with \(e^{-st}\), and \(e^{-st}\) is an eigenfunction of differentiation (§3 Ex 2, §6.2). The Laplace transform is therefore "project the signal onto the eigenfunctions of \(\frac{d}{dt}\) and keep the coefficients", exactly §1's change of basis, run on a function space (and exactly the same sentence you'll read again in §9 about Fourier, on the imaginary axis). That's why differentiation gets so much simpler in the new coordinates.

The payoff, derived by integration by parts, do this once yourself and the formula is permanent:

\[ \mathcal{L}\{y'\}=\int_0^\infty e^{-st}y'\,dt=\big[e^{-st}y\big]_0^\infty+s\int_0^\infty e^{-st}y\,dt=sY(s)-y(0),\qquad \mathcal{L}\{y''\}=s^2Y(s)-sy(0)-y'(0) \]

Derivatives in \(t\) become multiplication by \(s\), with the initial conditions appearing automatically in the boundary term. One IVP becomes

\[ \underbrace{ay''+by'+cy=g}_{\text{calculus problem}}\ \Longrightarrow\ (as^2+bs+c)\,Y(s)=(\text{IC terms})+G(s)\quad\text{· one algebra line} \]
The characteristic polynomial just moved house. That \(as^2+bs+c\) is the same quadratic §6.2 solved for \(r\). Its roots are now the poles of \(Y(s)\). The Laplace transform didn't invent a new method; it re-derived the characteristic equation while also absorbing the initial conditions and the forcing in one stroke. The partial-fraction numerators turn out to be the constants \(c_1,c_2\) of §6's method, same answer, one fewer "guess."

2. The table and how to derive each line #

\(f(t),\ t\ge0\)\(F(s)\)where it comes from

Two rows deserve ownership beyond memorization: s-shifting (so \(e^{-t}\cos2t\) is \(\frac{s}{s^2+4}\) with \(s\to s+1\): every damped oscillation is one shift from a clean one) and t-shifting (a delay in time is a factor \(e^{-cs}\) in \(s\)). The table is small and generative, re-derive one line a day and within a week the chapter has no magic in it.

3. Steps, impulses, delays: the reason Laplace exists #

§6's undetermined coefficients needs a tame \(g(t)\). Real inputs are switches, kicks, and packets: \(u(t-c)\) turns on at \(t=c\); \(\delta(t-c)\) is the unit impulse, a hammer strike, a sampled spike, the thing you feed a system to learn it. The t-shift rule handles both mechanically; and combining \(\mathcal{L}\{f*g\}=FG\) with the impulse gives the identity that is systems theory:

\[ Y(s)=H(s)G(s)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad y(t)=(g*h)(t)=\int_0^t g(\tau)\,h(t-\tau)\,d\tau,\qquad h=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{H\} \]

\(H(s)=Y(s)/G(s)\) is the transfer function, the whole system packaged as a rational function; \(h\), its inverse transform, is the impulse response: what the system outputs when kicked once. Know one, you know the other. §6's variation-of-parameters formula was quietly computing this same convolution with the Green's function. The two chapters are one theorem with different hats. And every block-diagram box, filter spec, and PID-tuning conversation you'll ever meet is a rational \(H(s)\) wearing marketing copy.

4. Poles, partial fractions, and stability #

Solving an IVP is a four-step loop: transform each term → collect \(Y(s)\) algebraically → partial fractions → invert term-by-term from the table. The marks live in step three; the three denominator shapes you'll meet:

\[ \frac{Ps+Q}{(s-r_1)(s-r_2)}=\frac{A}{s-r_1}+\frac{B}{s-r_2};\qquad \frac{P}{(s-r)^2}=\frac{A}{s-r}+\frac{B}{(s-r)^2};\qquad \frac{Ps+Q}{s^2+bs+c}=\text{complete the square}\to\text{damped sine/cosine} \]

The cover-up method finds \(A\) at a simple pole \(r_1\) in one second: \(A=\lim_{s\to r_1}(s-r_1)Y(s)\). It fails silently on repeated roots, split off one factor at a time there.

Stability = pole location, and this is §7 with new glasses. Each simple pole \(p\) contributes an \(e^{pt}\) to the inverse transform, so

\[ \text{all poles of }H(s)\text{ in the left half-plane }(\operatorname{Re}p\lt0)\iff\text{every response decays}\iff\text{stable} \]

The \(j\omega\) axis is the stability boundary. A pole at \(-\sigma+i\omega\) gives a sinusoid of frequency \(\omega\) inside an envelope \(e^{-\sigma t}\): distance from the axis = decay rate = \(-\operatorname{Re}\lambda\) in §7's portrait (Visual 11 draws both, literally). Poles on the axis: marginal, sustained oscillation (§7's center); right half-plane: growth, unstable. And the reason this isn't a new fact: for \(x'=Ax\), transforming gives \(X(s)=(sI-A)^{-1}x_0\), whose poles solve \(\det(sI-A)=0\). The poles ARE the eigenvalues. §7 and §8 were never different chapters.

6. Worked examples + numpy #

Worked 1 · the basic IVP: y′+2y=e⁻ᵗ, y(0)=0 (the same ODE as §6 Ex 1 with a different initial condition; two methods, one structure)

Transform: \(sY+2Y=\frac1{s+1}\Rightarrow Y=\frac1{(s+1)(s+2)}\). Partial fractions: \(\frac1{(s+1)(s+2)}=\frac{A}{s+1}+\frac{B}{s+2}\); cover-up: \(A=\frac1{-1+2}=1\), \(B=\frac1{-2+1}=-1\). So \(Y=\frac1{s+1}-\frac1{s+2}\) and \(y=e^{-t}-e^{-2t}\). Read the answer: poles at \(-1\) and \(-2\), one contributed by the forcing, one by the system. That's §6's \(y_p+y_h\) visible directly in the denominator, no guessing at forms. Sanity: \(y(0)=0\) ✔, \(y\to0\) ✔ (both poles left half-plane). Partial fractions is ~80% of the work on any Laplace exam question, get fast at it.

Worked 2 · a step forcing buys a piecewise answer for free

\(y'+y=u(t-1),\ y(0)=0\). Transform: \((s+1)Y=\frac{e^{-s}}{s}\Rightarrow Y=e^{-s}\cdot\frac1{s(s+1)}\). Partial-fraction the non-shifted part: \(\frac1{s(s+1)}=\frac1s-\frac1{s+1}\), inverse \(=1-e^{-t}\). By the t-shifting rule (\(e^{-cs}F(s)\leftrightarrow u(t-c)f(t-c)\)): \[ y(t)=u(t-1)\big(1-e^{-(t-1)}\big)=\begin{cases}0,&t\lt1\\[2pt]1-e^{-(t-1)},&t\ge1\end{cases} \] Dead until the switch flips, then the standard step response, delayed by exactly one. This is Laplace's raison d'être: §6 would handle this as two separate IVPs matched at \(t=1\); here it's one algebra line and \(e^{-cs}\) is the delay. (The same \(e^{-cs}\) models network latency, digital pipelines, and transmission lines, one table row, an entire field of engineering.)

Worked 3 · resonance seen as a double pole: y″+4y=cos2t, zero ICs

\((s^2+4)Y=\frac{s}{s^2+4}\Rightarrow Y=\frac{s}{(s^2+4)^2}\). Recognize (or table-row: differentiate \(\frac{\omega}{s^2+\omega^2}\) w.r.t. \(s\) and adjust) \(\frac{s}{(s^2+\omega^2)^2}\leftrightarrow\frac{t}{2\omega}\sin\omega t\), so with \(\omega=2\): \(y=\frac t4\sin2t\). §6 Ex 4's linearly growing amplitude, without the guessing game. The mechanism is now visible: \(Y\) has double poles at \(\pm2i\), the forcing's frequency landed exactly on the system's. Repeated pole ⇒ the \(t\)-multiplication ⇒ resonance. §6's awkward "multiply your guess by \(t\)" rule was always a statement about pole multiplicity.

Worked 4 · the impulse response, and why it contains the whole system

\(y''+3y'+2y=\delta(t)\), zero ICs: \((s^2+3s+2)Y=1\Rightarrow Y=\frac1{(s+1)(s+2)}=\frac1{s+1}-\frac1{s+2}\Rightarrow h(t)=e^{-t}-e^{-2t}\). This \(h\) is the system: response to any input \(g\) is \(g*h\) (§8.3). Physical reads: \(h(0^+)=0\) but \(h'(0^+)=1\), a unit impulse injects velocity, never position; and \(h\) decays because both poles (\(-1,-2\)) are left-half-plane. Compare Worked 1: same \(Y\), different story, a forcing of \(e^{-t}\) into this system produced exactly \(e^{-t}*h\), computed identically. The transfer-function view and the algebraic view are one view.

Worked 5 · the canonical transfer function (Visual 10 in formula form)

Unity-DC-gain second-order system: \(H(s)=\dfrac{\omega_0^2}{s^2+2\zeta\omega_0 s+\omega_0^2}\), mass-spring-damper (\(m=1,k=\omega_0^2,c=2\zeta\omega_0\)), §6.2's equation packaged. Poles: \(s=-\zeta\omega_0\pm\omega_0\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}\). ζ < 1: complex conjugate pair → underdamped step response with overshoot \(M_p=e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\) (the spiral of §7); ζ = 1: repeated real pole → critical (fastest, no overshoot); ζ > 1: two real poles → overdamped creep. Settling time ≈ \(4/(\zeta\omega_0)\), again "distance from the axis."

History. Fourier's 1807 memoir and 1822 Théorie analytique de la chaleur claimed that essentially any temperature profile is a sum of sines and cosines, a scandal. Lagrange objected (having argued a version of the impossibility for decades; corners can't be smooth waves, he said, and he was wrong in the limit but right that something had to be proved); the resulting argument forced Dirichlet to state actual convergence conditions (1829), and Riemann invented his integral decades later to fix Fourier integration rigorously. Gibbs (1899), after Wilbraham (1848!) noticed it, quantified the ~9% overshoot near a jump that no number of terms removes. Carleson's 1966 theorem (\(L^2\) functions have Fourier series converging almost everywhere) was called one of the hardest results of the century. Cooley–Tukey (1965) made the discrete version fast; the paper launched digital signal processing, with it: streaming audio, JPEG, MRI, and modem handshake tones.

1. The formulas and why they are just Gram–Schmidt #

\[ f(x)\sim \frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^\infty\left(a_n\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}+b_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right),\qquad a_n=\frac1L\int_{-L}^{L}f(x)\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx,\quad b_n=\frac1L\int_{-L}^{L}f(x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx \]
\[ \text{Complex form:}\quad f\sim\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_n\,e^{in\pi x/L},\qquad c_n=\frac1{2L}\int_{-L}^{L}f(x)\,e^{-in\pi x/L}\,dx \]

The derivation is one §1 sentence. Put an inner product on functions: \(\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{-L}^{L}f\,g\,dx\), the integral of a product is a dot product (§1's preview). The trig family is an orthogonal set:

\[ \int_{-L}^{L}\cos\frac{m\pi x}L\cos\frac{n\pi x}L\,dx=\begin{cases}0,&m\ne n\\ L,&m=n\ne0\end{cases},\qquad \int_{-L}^{L}\sin\frac{m\pi x}L\cos\frac{n\pi x}L\,dx=0\ \ \forall m,n \]

Prove it with the product-to-sum identities in three lines each (do it now, the \(\sin\perp\cos\) fact is just "odd function on a symmetric interval integrates to 0"). Then to extract \(a_n\): take the inner product of the whole series with \(\cos\frac{n\pi x}L\). Every other term dies by orthogonality; you're left with \(a_n\cdot L=\langle f,\cos_n\rangle\). That's the Euler formula. a projection, \(a_n=\langle f,\cos_n\rangle/\langle\cos_n,\cos_n\rangle\), the infinite-dimensional twin of Gram–Schmidt (§1.3) and the same move §8 made with \(e^{-st}\). Sines and cosines are not a magical family; they are an orthonormal basis that happens to fit \(\frac{d}{dx}\): the eigenfunctions of differentiation on periodic functions (\(\frac{d}{dx}e^{inx}=\,in\,e^{inx}\)). §0's program, executed at last: in this basis, differentiation is diagonal, multiply mode \(n\) by \(in\).

Symmetry shortcuts = free integrals. \(f\) even \(\Rightarrow\) \(b_n=0\) (cosine-only); \(f\) odd \(\Rightarrow\) \(a_n=0\) (sine-only), because even×odd is odd and odd functions integrate to 0 over \([-L,L]\). Half-range expansions: given \(f\) only on \((0,L)\), extend evenly → pure cosine series, or oddly → pure sine series, the same formula on \((0,L)\), two different functions outside it, two different series. And half-wave symmetry (\(f(x+L)=-f(x)\), like the square wave) kills every even harmonic. Check all three before writing one integral; exams are built on this paragraph.

2. Convergence and the Gibbs phenomenon #

Dirichlet's conditions (piecewise monotone with finitely many jumps per period) suffice, and then the series converges to \(f(x)\) wherever \(f\) is continuous and to the midpoint of the jump otherwise: \(\frac{f(x^+)+f(x^-)}2\). A series of smooth functions can't produce a corner: it can only average across it. So the square wave at \(x=0\) converges to 0, not \(\pm1\): every such series splits the difference.

Gibbs: near a jump the partial sums overshoot by a fixed ≈ 8.949% of the jump height, forever, increasing \(N\) narrows the spike (width \(\sim1/N\)) but never lowers it (the scaled limit is the sine integral \(\mathrm{Si}\)). Not a bug in your coefficients: it's the structural refusal of smooth functions to converge uniformly to a discontinuous one. Visual 12 shows you the overshoot standing its ground at \(N=61\). Why a CS student cares: the same constant causes ringing artifacts around sharp edges after JPEG quantization, in over-sharpened photos, and in reconstructed CT/MRI slices; the mitigation in every real pipeline is a window (smooth the edge first, pay in transition width to stop Gibbs), negotiation, not solution.

3. Parseval: energy and the license to compress #

\[ \frac1L\int_{-L}^{L}|f(x)|^2\,dx=\frac{a_0^2}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\big(a_n^2+b_n^2\big)\qquad\text{(complex form: }\ \frac1{2L}\int|f|^2=\sum_{-\infty}^{\infty}|c_n|^2\text{)} \]

Parseval is the Pythagorean theorem in the orthogonal basis: total energy = sum of squared components (write it as \(\|f\|^2=\sum|\langle f,e_n\rangle|^2\) and it's §1's theorem verbatim). Two uses you'll actually make: (1) a free checksum, compute both sides, mismatch means a coefficient is wrong (Ex 3 works it on the square wave); (2) it licenses lossy compression: discard the harmonics with the smallest \(|c_n|\) and you provably lose the least energy. That is the entire mathematics of the JPEG quality slider and MP3 bitrates. And the decay ladder: jump ⇒ \(c_n\sim1/n\); only a kink ⇒ \(\sim1/n^2\); \(C^k\) ⇒ \(\sim1/n^{k+1}\) (one integration by parts per derivative). Smooth signals are cheap; edges are expensive, which is exactly why cartoon-style images compress so well and film grain so badly.

5. Worked examples #

Worked 1 · the classic odd square wave (the one to memorize cold)

\(f=1\) on \((0,\pi)\), \(f=-1\) on \((-\pi,0)\), \(L=\pi\). Odd ⇒ \(a_n=0\) for free. Compute: \[ b_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f\sin nx\,dx=\frac2\pi\int_0^{\pi}\sin nx\,dx=\frac2\pi\cdot\frac{1-\cos n\pi}{n}=\frac{2\big(1-(-1)^n\big)}{n\pi}=\begin{cases}\dfrac4{n\pi},&n\ \text{odd}\\[4pt]0,&n\ \text{even}\end{cases} \] \[ \boxed{\,f(x)\sim\frac4\pi\left(\sin x+\frac{\sin 3x}{3}+\frac{\sin 5x}{5}+\cdots\right)\,} \] Even \(n\) die because of half-wave symmetry (§9.1's note), not by accident. First harmonic amplitude \(4/\pi\approx1.273>1\): the overshoot seed is already in \(b_1\). At \(x=\pi/2\): \(1=\frac4\pi(1-\frac13+\frac15-\cdots)\), the Leibniz series for \(\pi/4\), which is Fourier series casually evaluating itself.

Worked 2 · half-range cosine expansion of f(x)=x on (0,π)

Even extension ⇒ cosine series. \(a_0=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi x\,dx=\pi\). For \(n\ge1\), integrate by parts (\(u=x,\ dv=\cos nx\,dx\)): \[ a_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi x\cos nx\,dx=\frac2\pi\left[\frac{x\sin nx}{n}+\frac{\cos nx}{n^2}\right]_0^\pi=\frac{2\big((-1)^n-1\big)}{\pi n^2}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{-4}{\pi n^2},&n\ \text{odd}\\[4pt]0,&n\ \text{even}\end{cases} \] \[ f(x)\sim\frac\pi2-\frac4\pi\left(\cos x+\frac{\cos 3x}{9}+\frac{\cos 5x}{25}+\cdots\right) \] The even extension is a triangle wave: continuous, kinked at \(0,\pi\). Coefficients decay \(1/n^2\) where the square's decayed \(1/n\), one extra power of \(n\) bought by one extra degree of continuity. At \(x=0\) the series gives \(\frac\pi2-\frac4\pi\cdot\frac{\pi^2}8=0\)... matching the even extension's own value \(|0|=0\), the series converges to 0 ✔ and simultaneously proves \(\sum_{\text{odd}}1/n^2=\pi^2/8\). Smoothness ⇔ decay: the rule that makes gradients compress and edges resist.

Worked 3 · Parseval as checksum (and a bonus: Basel via Fourier)

Square wave: left side \(\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}1\,dx=2\) (since \(f^2\equiv1\)); right side \(\sum_{n\text{ odd}}b_n^2=\sum_{n\text{ odd}}\frac{16}{n^2\pi^2}\). Equate: \(\frac{16}{\pi^2}\sum_{\text{odd}}\frac1{n^2}=2\Rightarrow\sum_{\text{odd}}\frac1{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{8}\). a famous series, proved by computing an integral two ways. (The sawtooth version gives \(\sum 1/n^2=\pi^2/6\), the Basel problem.) Exam habit: finish any coefficient computation, then run Parseval; if the energies disagree, a \(b_n\) is wrong; it catches arithmetic errors that "look fine."

Worked 4 · solving the heat equation with the basis (the "why" behind the whole chapter)

Rod on \((0,\pi)\): \(u_t=u_{xx}\), \(u(0,t)=u(\pi,t)=0\), initial profile \(u(x,0)=f(x)=\sum b_n\sin nx\). Separate variables \(u=XT\): \(X''=\lambda X\) with \(X(0)=X(\pi)=0\). an eigenvalue problem for \(\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\), giving \(\lambda_n=-n^2\), \(X_n=\sin nx\); time then contributes \(e^{-n^2t}\) per mode, so \[ u(x,t)=\sum_{n}b_n\,e^{-n^2t}\sin nx \] Every mode evolves independently and high modes die like \(e^{-n^2t}\): the heat equation smooths, hard and fast, and the Fourier coefficients are why. Wave equation instead gives \(e^{\pm int}\) (oscillation, no decay). This is §0's thesis, diagonalize the operator, applied to a PDE: the sine basis diagonalizes \(d^2/dx^2\) exactly as eigenvectors diagonalize a matrix (§5). §9 wasn't the end of the material; it was the last chapter of linear methods.

6. Bridge: Fourier transform → DFT → FFT #

Let \(L\to\infty\): the frequency spacing \(\Delta\omega=\pi/L\to0\), the discrete sum of harmonics becomes an integral, and the Fourier transform appears (which is precisely §8's transform pinned to the imaginary axis \(s=i\omega\), one more reason the two chapters are twins): \[ \hat f(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)e^{-i\omega t}\,dt,\qquad f(t)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat f(\omega)e^{i\omega t}\,d\omega \]

The property list rewrites itself from §8: \(\widehat{f'}=i\omega\hat f\) (the eigenfunction fact, again); modulation/shift in \(t\) ↔ phase in \(\omega\); convolution ↔ multiplication (identical to §8.3, same theorem in \(e^{i\omega t}\) coordinates); multiplication in one domain ↔ convolution in the other (why sampling a signal = repeating its spectrum: aliasing, the Nyquist–Shannon theorem, and every "moiré on a plaid shirt" you've ever seen); Parseval's identity integral version.

Discretize: take \(N\) samples and evaluate on \(N\) equally spaced frequencies → the DFT \[ X_k=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x_n\,e^{-2\pi i kn/N}, \] a matrix–vector product with the Fourier matrix \(F\), whose entries are the \(N\)-th roots of unity. Direct cost: \(O(N^2)\). The FFT: split even/odd indices and \(F_N\) factors into two \(F_{N/2}\) copies plus a diagonal of cheap "twiddle" roots: \(F_N=\begin{bmatrix}I&D\\ I&-D\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}F_{N/2}&0\\0&F_{N/2}\end{bmatrix}\) (up to reindexing), so \(T(N)=2T(N/2)+O(N)=O(N\log N)\). A fast Fourier transform is fast because a matrix factorization was hiding inside the sum, the same "LU of a structured matrix" idea as §2, executed on roots of unity. On \(N=10^6\): ~50,000× speedup, which is why the entire digital-signal world exists. NumPy's convention (docs): negative exponent forward, \(1/N\) only on the inverse; check it once so a phase flip never costs you an afternoon.

Uses. MP3/AAC = FFT filterbank + psychoacoustic masking (throw away the small \(|c_n|\) a human can't hear. Parseval's mercy) · JPEG = 2-D DCT (§9's cosine-only half-range series of Worked 2, discretized) + quantize the high frequencies · spectrograms and Shazam fingerprints (hashes of FFT peak pairs) · filtering: denoise, equalize, band-limit by editing coefficients, the demo above · solving PDEs mode by mode (Worked 4) · fast convolution and big-integer/polynomial multiplication (\(O(N\log N)\), used in real crypto libraries) · the graph Fourier transform: eigenvectors of the Laplacian (§4's spectral row) generalize "frequency" to networks, spectral clustering and GNNs are §9 transplanted onto graphs · quantum computing: momentum basis = Fourier transform of position basis.
Pitfalls. (1) The \(a_0/2\): the average value of the wave is \(a_0/2\), and the convention exists so the \(a_n\) formula works at \(n=0\), first thing a grader checks. (2) Write \(1-\cos n\pi=1-(-1)^n\) explicitly; "even terms vanish" is worth knowing cold and citing. (3) Complex coefficients: \(c_n=(a_n-ib_n)/2\), the factor ½ and the sign of the imaginary part break every downstream Parseval check when dropped. (4) \(\int_{-L}^{L}\cos^2\frac{n\pi x}{L}dx=L\), not \(2L\), a factor-2 error invisible to the eye and fatal to coefficients; Parseval (Ex 3) catches it. (5) Sampling: the Nyquist rule, sample below \(2f_{\max}\) and high frequencies impersonate low ones (aliasing, and the demo's \(1/N\) convention note).

A. The graphics pipeline #

A vertex starts in model space and ends in clip space through a chain of linear (affine, projective) maps: \[ \underbrace{\text{model}}_{\text{rotate+scale+translate}}\ \to\ \underbrace{\text{world}}_{\text{camera position}}\ \to\ \underbrace{\text{view}}_{\text{camera look}}\ \to\ \underbrace{\text{projection}}_{\text{perspective, }\det=0\text{ crush }3D\to2D} \] Each is a 4×4 homogeneous matrix (§3 Ex 4); the GPU multiplies them into one MVP matrix once per frame (§2.1's composition), then applies it to every vertex. "factor once, apply many." Back in Visual 3, the Project preset (\(\det=0\)) is the orthographic camera flattening the scene; Shear is a skew; the eigenvector lines are the invariant axes. And an oriented bounding box (§5.1's powers of the covariance matrix / spectral theorem): the PCA axes of a mesh's vertices are its eigenvectors, a tight-fit box for collision detection, computed by one eigen-decomposition with no search. §1's orthogonality is why normals transform by \((M^{-1})^T\): a non-orthogonal model matrix shears your lighting if you forget.

B. PageRank, at scale #

The web's link matrix \(M\) (column-stochastic, §4.6) has dominant eigenvalue exactly \(\lambda_1=1\) by construction. PageRank is its \(\lambda_1\) eigenvector, computed by power iteration (Visual 5), not by diagonalizing, because a \(30\text{ billion}\times30\text{ billion}\) matrix can't be: but power iteration only needs a matrix-vector product, and \(M\) is sparse, so each iteration is cheap. Convergence rate \(|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|^k\): the second eigenvalue's size is the cost of the whole ranking. This is why the actual algorithm adds damping \(G=\alpha M+(1-\alpha)\tfrac1n ee^T\) with \(\alpha\approx0.85\), It shrinks \(|\lambda_2|\), manufacturing the eigenvalue gap that Visual 5's rotation case lacked. The single most consequential computation in CS history is "find the dominant eigenvector". §4's abstract object, run on the planet.

C. Audio & image filtering #

In Visual 12, keeping the first N harmonics is exactly an ideal low-pass filter. The Gibbs ringing you see at the square wave's jump is the same artifact as the halos around sharp edges in an over-sharpened photo, and the reason real filters use windows (Hamming/Kaiser): trade a slightly wider transition band for killing the ringing. Gibbs, negotiated with, not solved. The FFT (§9.6) does the decomposition in \(O(N\log N)\); the demo in §9 multiplies by a mask and inverse-transforms. JPEG: cut the image into 8×8 blocks, apply the 2-D DCT (the half-range cosine series of §9 Worked 2, discretized, even symmetry, so sines vanish), then quantize the high-frequency coefficients hard (divide by a frequency-weighted table and round). That rounding is the lossy compression, and Parseval (§9.3) is why it's tolerable: the discarded coefficients were the smallest-energy components. The "quality" slider is the size of the divisor. §8's RC-circuit pole and §9's harmonic cut are the same low-pass idea in the two domains. Visual 11's pole is an RC filter's cutoff.

D. Recommender systems via SVD #

The user × movie rating matrix \(R\) is non-square, huge, and 99% empty, plain eigen-decomposition is out (§5's "square + independent eigenvectors"), but SVD always applies. The truncated SVD \(R_k=\sum_{i\le k}\sigma_iu_iv_i^T\) (Eckart–Young, §5.5) forces \(R\approx\) (users × latent factors)·(factors × movies), and each latent factor is a taste dimension ("how much sci-fi," "how much arthouse"). Predict an unseen entry by reconstructing from the top \(k\), exactly the §5.5 rank-compression demo scaled up, and the mathematical core of the 2009 Netflix Prize winner (a refined SVD-family method). The same \(U\Sigma V^T\) that Visual 6 morphs is the one that finds you movies. §5's "abstract generalization" is a working product.

E. Control & stability #

A mass-spring-damper \(my''+cy'+ky=F(t)\) is §6.2 and §7 and §8 simultaneously. Overdamped (two real negative roots) / underdamped (complex pair → spiral, Visual 9/10) / undamped (pure imaginary → center). A controller wants the closed-loop poles left of the axis and a fast-but-not-oscillatory step response, i.e. tune \(\zeta\) just below 1 (Visual 10's sweet spot: small overshoot, fastest settle). Poles in LHP ⇔ stable (§8.4) is the frequency-domain restatement of the trace/det test (§7.2) is the eigenvalue condition (§4): one fact, three vocabularies, exactly the §0 thesis. In discrete systems (a flight computer, a game loop at fixed dt) the eigenvalue is measured in the \(z\)-plane, and "stable" becomes "inside the unit circle \(|z|<1\)". §6's numerical-stability demo (\(|1+h\lambda|<1\)) is this rule to one decimal place.

Eigenvalues/vectors?\(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\) → \((A-\lambda I)v=0\) → check \(Av=\lambda v\). Triangular? read the diagonal. 2×2? use \(\lambda^2-(\operatorname{tr})\lambda+\det\). Verify \(\sum\lambda=\operatorname{tr}\), \(\prod\lambda=\det\). (§4)
Diagonalize / \(A^k\)?Need \(n\) independent eigenvectors (distinct \(\lambda\) or symmetric). Stack \(P\), check \(\det P\neq0\). \(A^k=PD^kP^{-1}\). (§5)
2nd-order ODE?Characteristic equation \(ar^2+br+c=0\); three root cases (§6.2). For \(g\neq0\): \(y_h\) first, guess \(y_p\) by family, multiply by \(t\) if it overlaps \(y_h\) (resonance), then apply ICs. (§6)
System \(x'=Ax\) / classify?Eigen-decompose → \(c_1e^{\lambda_1t}v_1+c_2e^{\lambda_2t}v_2\). Stability: sign of \(\operatorname{Re}\lambda\), or 2×2 shortcut: \(\det<0\)=saddle; \(\det>0,\operatorname{tr}<0\)=stable (node if \(\operatorname{tr}^2\!\ge\!4\det\) else spiral). (§7)
Laplace?Transform (write \(sY-y(0)\) etc. mechanically) → solve for \(Y(s)\) → partial fractions (the real work) → invert by table. Step/delta forcing → \(e^{-cs}\) t-shift rule. Poles left half-plane ⇔ stable. (§8)
Fourier?Check even/odd first (kills half the coefficients). Compute Euler integrals; note \(1-(-1)^n\). Half-range ⇒ extend evenly (cosine) or oddly (sine). State convergence at jumps = midpoint. Parseval as a checksum. (§9)

2. 90-minute self-timed drill #

The five problems
  1. (15 min) \(A=\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\1&3\end{bmatrix}\): eigen-decompose, write \(A=PDP^{-1}\), and compute \(A^5\) exactly.
  2. (20 min) Solve \(y''+2y'+5y=0\). Then build the matrix \(A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-5&-2\end{bmatrix}\), find its eigenvalues, and classify the phase portrait, and explain in one sentence why the two answers must agree.
  3. (15 min) Solve by Laplace: \(y'+3y=\delta(t-1),\ y(0)=0\). Where is the pole, and what does it say about stability?
  4. (25 min) \(f(x)=x^2\) on \((-\pi,\pi)\): find its Fourier series. State whether it's even/odd, which coefficients vanish, compute the rest, and evaluate \(\sum 1/n^2\) using Parseval.
  5. (15 min) Out loud, no notes: give one concrete CS application of §4, §5, §7, §8, §9, and name which specific fact (eigenvalue / pole / coefficient) each application hinges on.
Solutions (attempt first!)

1. \(\lambda=4,2\); \(v=(1,1),(1,-1)\). \(P=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}\), \(A^5=P\,\mathrm{diag}(4^5,2^5)P^{-1}=\tfrac12\begin{bmatrix}1024+32&1024-32\\1024-32&1024+32\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}528&496\\496&528\end{bmatrix}\). (Symmetric ⇒ orthogonal eigenbasis, eigen-directions are the 45° axes. Visual 3's "Symmetric" preset is the same lesson, different eigenvalues.)
2. \(r^2+2r+5=0\Rightarrow r=-1\pm2i\Rightarrow y=e^{-t}(C_1\cos2t+C_2\sin2t)\). System: \(\operatorname{tr}=-2,\det=5,\operatorname{tr}^2-4\det=4-20<0\) → complex, \(\operatorname{Re}=-1<0\) → stable spiral. They agree because the scalar characteristic equation is the system's (\(\lambda\) of \(A\) = root of the ODE). §6/§7's equivalence, Visual 10 at \(\zeta=1/\sqrt5\).
3. \((s+3)Y=e^{-s}\Rightarrow Y=\frac{e^{-s}}{s+3}\Rightarrow y=u(t-1)e^{-3(t-1)}\). Pole at \(s=-3\), left half-plane → stable; the impulse at \(t=1\) just restarts the exponential decay, delayed by 1 (§8.3's delay rule).
4. Even ⇒ \(b_n=0\). \(a_0=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi x^2dx=\frac{2\pi^2}3\)… full: \(a_n=\frac{4(-1)^n}{n^2}\). \(x^2=\frac{\pi^2}3+\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{4(-1)^n}{n^2}\cos nx\). Parseval: \(\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}x^4dx=\frac{a_0^2}2+\sum a_n^2\) → \(\frac{2\pi^4}5=\frac{2\pi^4}9+16\sum\frac1{n^4}\) (gives \(\sum1/n^4=\pi^4/90\)); evaluating the series at \(x=\pi\) instead gives \(\sum1/n^2=\pi^2/6\).
5. Sample answers: §4 dominant eigenvector = PageRank (§10B); §5 truncated SVD = Netflix recommender (§10D); §7 sign of Re λ = SIR threshold R₀ (§7 Ex 2); §8 pole location = PID stability / filter cutoff (§10C,E); §9 DCT coefficients = JPEG (§10C).
